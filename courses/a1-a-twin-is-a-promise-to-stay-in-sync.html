Learn Digital Twins with Phoebe · Leader session 1 of 6

A twin is a promise to stay in sync

A 3D model of a building is a picture. A digital twin is a promise: this model describes this particular building as it is today, it is kept up to date with data from the building, and someone will use it to decide something. Kittiwake Hall, a constructed six-storey teaching building whose facilities team is building a thermal twin to plan heating, is the case for all sixteen sessions; every Kittiwake number is generated in the page, not observed. Tonight is what a twin is, what the promise costs to keep, and how to tell a twin that keeps it from one that only looks like it does. Nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Kittiwake Hall, the week the twin arrives

Kittiwake Hall is constructed: six floors of seminar rooms, a heat meter per floor, one temperature sensor per floor, a weather feed, and a set of drawings from when the building was designed. The facilities lead wants to turn the heating down at night and asks for a twin that can say what that would save. A data analyst who came from a retail warehouse gets the ticket. What she has to build is not a dashboard and not a 3D model: it is a small physics model of each floor that has to keep agreeing with the building, hour after hour, or stop being trusted. The six leader sessions are about deciding with such a model; the ten practitioner sessions are about building one.

LiveWhat a twin is, in one definition4 min▶

The most careful public definition comes from the National Academies (2024), adapted from an aerospace position paper by AIAA and AIA (2020). Paraphrased: a set of virtual information constructs that mimics the structure, context and behaviour of a physical system, is dynamically updated with data from its physical twin, has a predictive capability, and informs decisions that realize value.

Read it as four tests. Is there one particular physical thing? Is the model updated from that thing's data, not just built once? Can it predict something? Does someone decide with it? A model that fails the second test is a simulation, which can be excellent, but it is not making the promise. The report says it plainly: a digital twin is "more than just simulation and modeling."

Real world

The AIAA and AIA (2020) paper names the essential elements as a virtual representation, a physical realization and a transfer of data between the two, and draws the consequence: to have a digital twin requires a physical asset. It follows the asset through phases it calls As Designed, As Built, As Used and As Maintained. Leader session 3 is about the distance between the first two.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Asset. The one physical thing the twin is of: here, one floor of Kittiwake Hall. Not a type of floor; this floor.
  • State. What the asset is doing now: the room temperature, and the temperature of the building's mass, which nobody measures.
  • Parameter. How the asset behaves: how fast a floor loses heat, how much heat its concrete stores. Practitioner session 3 fits them.
  • Calibration. Choosing parameter values so the model agrees with past measurements.
  • Data assimilation. Correcting the model's state with each new measurement, weighted by how much each is trusted. The Kalman filter (Kalman 1960) is the classic method; practitioner session 4 does it by hand.
  • Innovation. The surprise: what the sensor read minus what the twin predicted. A twin that stays in sync has small, patternless innovations.
  • Constructed. Kittiwake Hall and every number about it are invented so that the truth is knowable. The research and standards are real and cited.
Part 1 · covers the four parts and the loop that ties them

Asset, model, data link, decision

15 min live

Every twin has the same four parts. What makes it a twin rather than a model is the loop: data flows from the asset into the model, the model predicts, a person (or a controller) decides, and the decision changes the asset, which produces the next data. Break any link and the promise lapses, usually without anyone noticing.

LiveWhat each part costs, and who owns it6 min▶

The asset is owned by facilities. It changes on its own schedule: a window replaced, a radiator swapped, a floor insulated. Each change is a change to the thing the twin claims to mirror.

The data link is usually owned by nobody in particular, and that is its risk. Sensors drift, meters are replaced, a feed goes quiet over a holiday. Sensor and clock problems belong to a sibling course, learn IoT data; here the question is whether the model is told.

The model is owned by whoever built it, often a consultant who has left. Its parameters came from somewhere: the drawings, a calibration week, a vendor default.

The decision is owned by a person with a budget. The twin is worth exactly what it changes about that decision, and leader session 2 argues that this, not realism, sets how detailed it needs to be.

Self-studyWhere the idea came from, and the dispute about it4 min▶

The usual origin story credits Michael Grieves, who in Grieves and Vickers (2017) dates the model to 2002, a slide called "Conceptual Ideal for PLM" presented for a product lifecycle management centre, without a name at the time. Accounts differ on who coined the term "digital twin" around 2010, and at least one published critique argues the 2002 claim lacks contemporaneous documentary evidence. The honest version: the idea of a physical product paired with a virtual one has been in engineering since at least the early 2000s; who said it first is disputed, and nothing about using a twin well depends on settling it.

Self-studyTwins that are not about buildings3 min▶

The National Academies (2024) definition covers natural, engineered and social systems: an aircraft engine, a patient, a forest, a city's traffic. The four parts and the loop are the same; what changes is how often the asset changes, how much it costs to measure, and who gets hurt if the twin is wrong. City-scale data and residents' consent live in learn smart city.

LiveReading the three rungs5 min▶

From the drawings. 4.450 C off the true room, and 32.9 percent low on the heat the setback week really used. Every real floor loses heat faster than designed, so a twin that believes the drawings believes the building is cheaper to heat than it is.

Calibrated. Fit to one week of readings, it is 0.482 C off and 1.7 percent high. Most of the value arrived here, in the parameters.

Kept in sync. With a correction every hour, 0.218 C off, better than the sensors alone (0.404 C). The plan barely moves, because a week-ahead plan depends on how the building behaves, not on this morning's temperature.

Two more options make it worse on purpose. "Trust the sensors fully" keeps the error near the sensors' own (0.403 C) and makes the twin's uncertainty band lie. "Unlogged retrofit" changes floor 4 without telling the twin: floor 4's plan goes 63.1 percent wrong, and only the surprises show it.

Self-studyWhy "real-time" is not the promise3 min▶

A feed that updates every second can still be wrong about what it feeds. The promise is not speed; it is that the model and the asset are checked against each other often enough for the decision. Kittiwake plans heating a week ahead, so hourly is plenty. The National Academies (2024) ask for verification, validation and uncertainty quantification to be a continual process that adapts as the physical counterpart changes. Leader session 4 takes "real-time" claims apart.

Part 3 · build-along

Five questions to put to any twin

★ 17 min · everyone builds

You will leave with a one-page check you can run on any twin before acting on what it says, whether a vendor shows it to you or your own team built it. The figure is the order to ask in: each question guards one link of the loop.

Ask which asset, and which version

Write the asset's name and the date of the last physical change the twin knows about. If the answer is "the design", write that down: Kittiwake's design-spec twin was 4.45 C off.

Ask which decision it will change

One sentence: "we will use the twin to decide X by date Y". If nobody can say, the twin has no purpose to be fit for, and leader session 2 cannot help you size it.

Ask how, and how often, it is updated

From which feeds, at what interval, and whether the state, the parameters, or both are updated. "Real-time" is not an answer to this question; an interval is.

Ask how anyone knows it still matches

Ask to see the surprises: the readings minus the predictions over the last month. If nobody looks at them, nobody would notice an unlogged change. In Kittiwake's bench the surprise on floor 4 jumped from +0.015 to +0.638 C while the room estimate still looked fine.

Ask how wide its band is, and whether it was checked

A twin that gives a single number for next week has hidden its uncertainty. A twin that gives a band should say how often reality fell inside it. Commit to a guess before session 7: Kittiwake's honest 95 percent band covered 96.8 percent; what do you think happens if the twin is told its sensors are perfect?

The habit to keep. When someone shows you a twin, do not ask how realistic it looks. Ask which asset, which decision, how it is updated, how anyone knows it still matches, and how wide its band is. A twin with no answer to the fourth is a model that used to be a twin.

Check yourself

Three questions

A team shows a detailed 3D model of a building, built from its drawings, with no data feed. Is it a digital twin by the National Academies (2024) definition?

The definition asks for a model of a particular system that is updated with data from it, predicts, and informs decisions. Detail and rendering speed are not on the list.

Kittiwake's design-spec twin (constructed) estimates the setback week's heat 32.9 percent low. What most likely went wrong?

The constructed floors lose 1.15 to 1.45 times the designed heat. Calibrated on one week of readings, the estimate is 1.7 percent high. Leader session 3 is about this gap.

After an unrecorded change to floor 4, Kittiwake's synced twin still tracks the room within 0.4 C. What should a leader ask to see?

The correction hides the change in the room estimate; the surprises it has to make do not. Floor 4's jumped from +0.015 to +0.638 C, flagging it and no other floor.

Before session 2

Homework

Twenty minutes, on one twin and on one decision.

Run the five questions on one real claim

Pick any public description of a digital twin, from any sector, and write one line per question. Do not judge the claim; mark which questions it leaves unanswered.

Name the decision

For an asset you know (a building, a machine, a fleet), write the one decision a twin of it would change, who makes it, and how often. Leader session 2 sizes the twin from that sentence.
