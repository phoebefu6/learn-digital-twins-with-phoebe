The as-built gap

The building you drew versus the one you have. Every building is put up a little differently from its drawings, and a twin that believes the drawings inherits that difference as a steady, one-directional error. Kittiwake Hall, the constructed six-storey teaching building that runs through all sixteen sessions, shows the size of it: every floor loses more heat than drawn, so a twin built from the drawings is 32.9 percent low on a week's heat and promises a bigger saving than the building delivers. Every Kittiwake number is generated in the page, not observed. Nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Kittiwake Hall, the day the drawings meet the meters

Kittiwake Hall is constructed: six floors of seminar rooms, a heat meter and a room sensor per floor, a weather feed, and a set of drawings from when the building was designed. Two sessions ago the facilities lead asked for a twin to plan a night setback; last session sized it at a simple two-node model per floor. Now the analyst has to fill that model with numbers. The obvious source is the drawings: wall build-ups, insulation thicknesses, the designed heat loss of every floor. They are tidy, signed off, and wrong in a direction that matters. Tonight is about how wrong, why, and what a week of the building's own readings does about it.

The size of the gap in Kittiwake's constructed bench: the real floors lose 1.19 to 1.42 times the heat the drawings say, so the design twin is 21.6 to 42.5 percent low on energy floor by floor, while a twin calibrated on one week of readings is within 3.5 percent. And a five-question as-built audit to run before you fund a twin.

LiveThe four phases, as the twin community names them3 min▶

A twin is a model of one particular asset. But a particular asset is not one thing over its life: it is drawn, then built, then used, then repaired and changed. A model that was faithful at one of those moments can be unfaithful at the next, and nothing in the model itself will say so.

Real world

The AIAA and AIA (2020) position paper, read at source, follows a twin through life-cycle phases it calls "As Designed", "As Built", "As Used" and "As Maintained". It also insists that a twin needs a physical asset and a transfer of data between the two. Put the two ideas together and the leader's question writes itself: which phase does this twin describe, and what data moved it there?

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • As-designed. The asset as the drawings and specifications describe it. Precise, documented, and a plan rather than a record.
  • As-built. The asset as it was actually put up, including every shortcut, substitution and site decision.
  • Heat-loss multiplier (kU). How much faster a real Kittiwake floor loses heat than its drawings say. 1.00 means exactly as drawn.
  • Thermal-mass multiplier (kC). How much heat the floor's concrete really stores, against the drawings.
  • Calibration. Choosing those multipliers so the model agrees with past readings. Practitioner session 3 does it by grid search.
  • Prior and evidence. What you believe before you measure (the drawings), and what the measurements say. Good practice lets the evidence move the prior, parameter by parameter.
  • Identifiability. Whether a given set of readings can pin a parameter down at all. Some cannot, however carefully you fit.
Part 1 · covers As Designed, As Built, As Used, As Maintained

Four phases of one building

12 min live

The drawings describe the building someone intended. The building on site is the one someone managed to put up, with the materials that arrived, in the weather they had. The difference is rarely a scandal; it is the ordinary friction of construction. For a twin it is decisive, because a model filled from the drawings is a model of the first phase being asked questions about the second.

LiveWhat changes between drawing and building6 min▶

Workmanship. Insulation boards that do not quite meet, a cavity with debris in it, a seal that was never finished behind a radiator. Each is small; together they decide how fast heat leaves a floor.

Substitutions. A specified product is unavailable, a cheaper or thinner one arrives, and the change is approved on site. Sometimes it reaches the record; often it does not.

Commissioning. The heating system is set up and tested at handover: valves balanced, controls checked, setpoints entered. What the commissioning engineer actually set is part of the as-built building, and it is easy to lose.

None of these needs a culprit. The point for a twin is direction: most of the friction in construction lets heat out rather than keeping it in, so a twin from the drawings is not just uncertain; it is biased, and the bias has a sign you can predict.

Real world

The AIAA and AIA (2020) paper treats As Built as its own phase rather than a footnote to As Designed, and it defines a twin as updated with data from its physical twin across the life cycle. Its line on the asset is blunt: "Hence to have a Digital Twin requires a physical asset." A drawing is not that asset; the building is.

Self-studyAs Used and As Maintained: the gap keeps moving3 min▶

Closing the as-built gap once is not the end. Once the building is used, people prop doors open, change a timetable, add a server room. Once it is maintained, a window is replaced, a radiator swapped, a floor insulated. Each is a new version of the asset the twin claims to mirror. Kittiwake's bench plants one such change, an unlogged insulation retrofit on floor 4, and the twin's heat estimate for that floor goes 63.1 percent wrong. Practitioner session 6 treats the change log as a data source; leader session 4 asks how a claim tells you which version it describes.

Self-studyWhy a warehouse analyst misses this2 min▶

In a warehouse, the schema is the record: the table's definition and its contents are the same thing. In a building the definition (the drawings) and the contents (the building) were made by different people at different times, and only one of them is measured. Treating the drawings like a schema, as the truth by construction, is the habit to drop. Leader session 5 takes the warehouse playbook apart in full.

LiveReading the table5 min▶

The true multiplier. At the canon seed the real floors lose 1.42, 1.19, 1.21, 1.41, 1.41 and 1.21 times the drawn heat. The drawings say 1.00 for all six.

The design twin's miss. 21.6 to 42.5 percent low per floor, 32.9 percent low for the building: it estimates 3,402 kWh for a week the building actually used 5,067. Every sign is negative. That is what a bias looks like, as opposed to noise.

The calibrated twin. Fitted to one week of readings, it recovers every multiplier within 0.08 and lands between -1.2 and +3.5 percent per floor, +1.7 percent for the building (5,152 kWh).

The end floors. Floors 1 and 6 have a roof or ground slab and lose 1.3 times more even in the drawings. The multiplier is on top of that, so a large multiplier on an end floor, like floor 1's 1.42, is the most heat the drawings leave out.

Self-studyWhy the design twin oversells the saving4 min▶

The decision Kittiwake's twin serves is a night setback: heat to 15 C at night and at weekends instead of 19. In the constructed truth that saves 20.1 percent (6,343 to 5,067 kWh). The calibrated twin says 21.5 percent. The design twin says 23.9 percent.

Why too high? A building that loses heat slowly, as the drawings believe, cools less overnight and needs less to recover in the morning, so a model of that building credits the setback with more. The error has a direction, and so does its effect on the business case: a twin from the drawings makes the saving look bigger than it is. In every seed the bench has run, the design twin overstates the saving and calibration brings it within about 1.5 points.

These are constructed numbers for one constructed building. They show the mechanism, not the size of savings anywhere real.

Self-studyWhere the method comes from3 min▶

Kittiwake's twin keeps the physics structure from the drawings (rooms and mass joined by heat paths) and takes the numbers from data. That is a grey-box model, and leader session 2 argued it is the right fidelity for a heating plan.

Real world

Bacher and Madsen (2011), in Energy and Buildings, identify models for the heat dynamics of a building as grey-box models: physical equations whose parameters are estimated from measured data. As reported in secondary summaries, they grow the model step by step and test each added part against the data before accepting it, applied to a test building. The lesson for a leader is the order of authority: the structure may come from the drawings, the numbers should come from the building.

Part 3 · build-along

Drawings as a prior, measurements as evidence

★ 17 min · everyone builds

The right way to hold the drawings is neither to trust them nor to throw them away. They are a prior: a reasonable first guess for every parameter. A week of readings is evidence, and evidence moves some parameters a lot and others hardly at all. You will leave with a one-page audit that asks, for a twin you are about to fund, which parameters the evidence has actually moved.

LiveCommissioning data is the cheapest evidence you will ever get4 min▶

The weeks around handover are the one time a building is measured with intent: systems are switched on deliberately, setpoints are known, nobody has yet moved in and propped a door open. Readings from that window are the evidence a twin's parameters most need, and they are routinely archived as a pass or fail certificate rather than kept as data.

Kittiwake's calibration week is the constructed version of that window: one ordinary week of hourly readings per floor fits every heat-loss multiplier within 0.08, with the fitted model 0.460 to 0.526 C from the sensors. Ask for the raw readings, not the certificate. How sensors, clocks and missing readings should be handled is the territory of learn IoT data.

Self-studyWhen to distrust the drawings, and when to distrust the fit4 min▶

Distrust the drawings for anything that depends on workmanship: heat loss, air leakage, how well a joint was sealed. These are where construction friction lives, and in Kittiwake every one of them points the same way.

Distrust the fit for anything the evidence could not see. Floor 3's thermal mass came out at 1.68 times the drawings when the truth is 0.89, and on floor 1 the fit said 1.62 against 0.85. A week of ordinary heating does not swing the building's mass hard enough to reveal it. The honest move is to keep the drawing value as the prior for that parameter and say so, rather than report a fitted number the data cannot support. Practitioner session 3 calls this identifiability.

Real world

ASHRAE Guideline 14 (editions 2002 and 2014, reported via secondary papers; the standard is paywalled) is commonly cited for calibration criteria: a model is often treated as calibrated when hourly CV(RMSE) is at most 30 percent and NMBE within plus or minus 10 percent, or monthly 15 and 5 percent. Garrett and New (2016) question whether those metrics are enough (reported). Notice what they measure: agreement with the meter, not whether each parameter was pinned. Kittiwake's floor 3 can match its meter well and still carry a mass value the week never tested.

Self-studyWho owns the as-built record3 min▶

The drawings have an owner, usually the design team, and a revision number. The as-built record often has neither: the contractor's marked-up set, the commissioning engineer's settings, the substitution approvals, each in a different place. A twin built on that record needs one person who can say which version it reflects and who must be told when it changes. Leader session 4 reads a twin claim for exactly this; practitioner session 8 assigns the owners. This is a working practice, not legal or compliance advice.

Ask which drawing revision the twin was built from

Write the revision and its date. If the answer is "the design set" and nothing later, write that down too: Kittiwake's design twin was 32.9 percent low on the setback week's heat for exactly that reason.

Ask what was measured after handover

Which readings, how many weeks, at what interval, and whether the raw data still exists or only a certificate does. One week of hourly readings was enough to pin Kittiwake's heat loss; none would have left the twin on the drawings.

Ask which parameters were fitted, and on what data

List them. For each, ask whether the data could actually see it: a fitted value the evidence could not pin, like floor 3's thermal mass at 1.68 against a true 0.89, is a guess with a decimal point.

Ask which were left at design values

This is the row most often blank. Every twin keeps some drawing values; the honest ones say which, and why that is acceptable for the decision. Kittiwake's twin keeps the room air capacity and assumes an average occupant load.

Ask what has changed since

Retrofits, replaced equipment, changed timetables. Each is a new version of the asset. If nobody can answer, the twin's as-built gap is quietly reopening; Kittiwake's unlogged retrofit on floor 4 put that floor's heat estimate 63.1 percent wrong.

The habit to keep. When a twin's numbers are presented, ask where each parameter came from: the drawings, a measurement, or a fit the data could not support. A twin from the drawings is not neutral; it is wrong in a direction you can predict, and usually in the direction that flatters the business case.

Check yourself

Three questions

Kittiwake's design twin (constructed) is low on the setback week's heat on every floor, by 21.6 to 42.5 percent. Why is every error the same sign?

A bias, not noise: construction friction lets heat out, so a twin that assumes 1.00 everywhere underestimates every floor. Calibrated on one week, the errors fall to -1.2 to +3.5 percent.

Calibration on one week of readings recovered every floor's heat-loss multiplier within 0.08, but put floor 3's thermal mass at 1.68 against a true 0.89. Why?

Some parameters are identifiable from a given set of readings and some are not. The honest twin keeps the drawing value as the prior for kC and says so, rather than trusting a fitted number the data never tested.

The design twin says a night setback saves 23.9 percent; the constructed truth is 20.1 percent. Why does a twin from the drawings oversell it?

The as-built gap carries into the decision. Calibrated, the twin says 21.5 percent. In every seed the bench has run, the design twin overstates the saving.
