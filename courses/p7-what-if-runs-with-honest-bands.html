What-if runs with honest bands

The facilities team at Kittiwake Hall, a constructed six-storey teaching building, wants to know what a night setback will do before anyone turns the dial. The twin can answer, but an answer without a band is a guess in a suit. Tonight you take one forecast apart: where its 95 percent band comes from, how to check it against the readings that arrive, why a band that is too narrow is the most dangerous output a twin can produce, and what the band honestly leaves out. Energy figures are constructed and are not investment advice.

Part 0

The question a twin exists to answer

Tracking the room is the twin's homework. The exam is a question nobody can measure yet: "if we run a 15 C night setback next week, what happens?". The twin answers by running itself forward under the changed schedule. Kittiwake's twin (constructed) answers twice: every midnight of week 2 it forecasts the next 24 hours with a band, and once, at the end of week 1, it plans the whole week under both schedules to price the saving. Both answers are only worth something if you can say how wrong they are allowed to be.

★ What you walk out with tonight. The what-if as a fork: one state, two schedules, two futures, one of which is never measured. The band taken apart into its three terms: state uncertainty, sensor noise and the thermostat's own noise. A habit: count coverage after the fact, every week, and treat 77 percent under a "95 percent" label as a defect. A clear list of what the band does not contain. And a short Python script that shows a band shrinking and lying when you assume a sensor ten times better than it is.

LiveWhy a forecast without a band is not an answer3 min▶

"The setback will use 5,152 kWh" sounds exact. It is one number from one model, and the person deciding has no way to tell whether the twin means "give or take 10 kWh" or "give or take 1,000". The band is the twin's statement of how far the world may land from its prediction. It is also a claim that can be checked: say 95 percent, and about 95 of every 100 readings that later arrive should fall inside. A twin that reports a band has agreed to be graded.

Real world

The National Academies (2024) report on digital twins lists a predictive capability and informing decisions among the defining features of a twin, and singles out uncertainty quantification as work still to be done. A what-if with a band is where those two meet: a prediction under a decision nobody has taken yet, with a stated, testable uncertainty.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • What-if. A forecast run under an input that differs from what actually happened or will happen by default: a new schedule, a new setpoint.
  • Counterfactual. The future that did not happen. In a real building it is never measured; in Kittiwake it exists only because the world is constructed.
  • Band. An interval around the forecast that the twin says will contain a stated share of the readings: here 95 percent, 1.96 standard deviations each side.
  • Nominal. The share the band claims (95 percent).
  • Coverage. The share it actually contained, counted after the readings arrived.
  • Propagation. Carrying the twin's uncertainty forward hour by hour through the model, as the Kalman filter does between readings (practitioner session 4).
Part 1 · covers the what-if and the counterfactual

A what-if is a forecast under a changed input

8 min live

The twin starts from the state the filter has kept in sync, then runs forward with no readings, its own thermostat and a schedule someone is considering. Run it twice from the same state, once per schedule, and the difference is the twin's estimate of what the change does. The figure is Kittiwake's week-ahead fork: one future is what the building really did, the other is a future nobody can visit.

LiveTwo what-ifs, two jobs4 min▶

The 24-hour forecast. Each midnight of week 2 the twin takes its filtered state for each floor and runs 24 hours ahead under the night setback, with its own copy of the thermostat deciding the heat hour by hour. No readings enter during those 24 hours. Then the real readings arrive and the band can be graded. Over the seven midnights and six floors that is 1,008 forecast hours.

The week-ahead plan. At hour 168 the twin runs the whole of week 2 twice from the same state: once under the setback, once under the normal schedule. The difference is its saving. In the constructed truth the setback took the building from 6,343 to 5,067 kWh, a 20.1 percent saving. The calibrated twin says 21.5 percent; the design-spec twin, built from the drawings, says 23.9 percent. Calibration did most of the work here (practitioner session 3), not the filter: a week-ahead plan runs on the parameters.

Real world

The AIAA and AIA (2020) position paper defines a twin as something that informs decisions that realize value, across the asset's life. A what-if is where that happens: the decision is taken on a future that the twin computed and nobody observed, so the twin's own calibration is all that stands between the plan and the building.

LiveWhy the twin runs its own thermostat2 min▶

Under the setback the heat is not an input any more: nobody has logged it, because it has not happened. The twin has to decide it the way the building would, from the setpoint and the room it predicts, 4 kW per degree below setpoint up to 20 kW. That makes the what-if a closed loop, and it is why the thermostat's noise ends up inside the band in Part 2. A what-if that replays last week's heat under next week's schedule is not a what-if; it is a mismatch.

Self-studyWhat the savings literature disagrees about4 min▶

Savings attributed to twins in published work vary widely and are disputed, and no single figure from that literature belongs on a slide. The disagreement has causes you can now name. Studies differ in the baseline (a counterfactual nobody measured, so it is itself a model), in what the twin is credited with (the setback saved the energy; the twin only helped choose it), in whether the twin was calibrated before the claim, and in whether any band was reported at all. Kittiwake shows the size of one of those effects in a constructed world: the same setback is worth 20.1, 21.5 or 23.9 percent depending on whose model you ask. When you read a savings claim, ask for the baseline, the model version that produced it, and the band. Leader session 4 turns this into a reading checklist.

Part 2 · covers where the band comes from

Where the band comes from

8 min live

The band is 1.96 times the standard deviation of the reading the twin expects: the square root of its predicted state variance plus the sensor noise it assumes. The state variance is carried forward hour by hour through the model, exactly as the Kalman filter carries it between readings, and while the thermostat is between off and full power it also carries the thermostat's noise, because the real thermostat acts on a noisy sensor. The figure draws the three terms and where each enters.

LiveThree terms, one square root4 min▶

State uncertainty. At midnight the filter hands over its state and its covariance P: how unsure it is of the room air and the mass. Each hour ahead, P is pushed through the model (A P A') and the unmodelled disturbance Q is added, so it grows. With no readings arriving, nothing shrinks it.

Controller noise. In the hours the thermostat is working (between 0 and 20 kW), the real building's heat depends on a noisy reading. The twin cannot know that noise, so it widens P by it. When the heater is off or flat out, a little sensor noise changes nothing and this term drops out.

Sensor noise. The band is for the reading, not for the true room, so the sensor's own noise is added at the end. This term is not estimated by anything in the forecast; it is the number in the config file. At Kittiwake's honest 0.4 C the band is 2.20 C wide on average and covers 96.8 percent of the readings (per floor 94.0 to 99.4 percent).

Real world

Kalman (1960) recast filtering and prediction in state-space form and showed that the error covariance obeys its own recursion. That recursion is the whole trick here: a forecast is the predict step run 24 times with no update, and the band is its covariance read off at each step.

LiveReading floor 1's Monday4 min▶

At the canon settings the chart starts from the filtered state at Monday midnight, 16.70 C, and the band is about 2.1 to 2.3 C wide all day. Twenty-two of the 24 readings land inside. The two that do not are worth reading. At 09:00 the forecast says 17.66 C and the sensor reads 16.44 C: the room recovered from the night more slowly than the twin thought. In practitioner session 1 the filter's one-hour-ahead prediction for the same hour was 17.42 C; nine hours of running blind cost a quarter of a degree more. At 19:00 the reading sits 1.15 C above the forecast as the evening setback begins.

Now pick 0.01 C. The band shrinks by about 40 percent and only 18 readings stay inside. The twin has not become better at forecasting; it has become surer of itself.

Self-studyThe closed-loop step, as the engine writes it4 min▶

In the hours the thermostat is between off and full power, its law (4 kW per degree below setpoint) is folded into the model, and the sensor noise it acts on is added to the process noise through the heat input's effect on the room and the mass (the column bq of the hourly map). The variable names follow dtw-live.js; read it there, then compare with the widget's script on this page.

whatIf, one hour of the bandif (q > 0 && q < qMax) { A = A - bq * kp // the loop: heat now depends on the room Qe = Q + (bq * kp) r (bq * kp)' // the thermostat's noisy reading enters } P = A P A' + Qe // carry the uncertainty one hour ahead sd = sqrt(P[room][room] + r) // add the sensor's own noise band = prediction +/- 1.96 * sd

This is pseudocode, not something to run. Drop the controller term and the band narrows in the working hours; drop r and it collapses to the state alone. The engine's self-test plants exactly that second fault and proves a check catches it.

Part 3 · covers checking a band, and what it leaves out

Checking a band

4 min live

A band is checked after the fact, by counting. Every reading that arrives either fell inside the band the twin issued for that hour or it did not. Over a week, the share inside should sit near the nominal 95 percent. A little above means the band is slightly wide; far below means the twin is claiming a certainty it does not have. The figure plots Kittiwake's four settings against that line.

LiveCoverage is a count, and it is never finished2 min▶

Kittiwake's honest band covers 96.8 percent: slightly conservative, which is the safe side to miss on. The anti-lever, 0.01 C, covers 77.4 percent while still calling itself 95. Its band is 1.28 C wide instead of 2.20, and it looks more professional on a chart, which is exactly the problem. A narrow band is not a precise twin; it is a claim, and the count says the claim is false. Run the count every week, per floor, because a building changes and a band that was honest in March can drift.

Real world

The National Academies (2024) call verification, validation and uncertainty quantification "an area of particular need" for digital twins, and conclude that it must be continual, adapting as the physical counterpart changes. A weekly coverage count per floor is the smallest working version of that.

LiveWhat this band does not include2 min▶

The band assumes the model's parameters are right. They are not known exactly. Practitioner session 3 showed that one week of data pins down each floor's heat loss (kU within 0.08) but not its thermal mass (floor 3: kC 1.68 found against 0.89 true). That uncertainty is nowhere in the 2.20 C. For a 24-hour room forecast it matters little, because the filter has just reset the state. For the saving it matters a lot: a week-ahead plan runs on the parameters, so a band on "21.5 percent" would need the parameter uncertainty propagated too. The honest sentence is "21.5 percent, from a twin whose band covers room readings; no band on the saving itself". Methods that add parameter uncertainty (Bayesian calibration, sensitivity analysis) exist and are named here only.

Self-studyForecast intervals in general3 min▶

A twin's band is one kind of forecast interval: model-based, propagated through physics. Empirical intervals, quantile methods and how to score interval forecasts in general belong to forecasting, and live in learn time-series forecasting. The one habit that transfers both ways: whatever produced the interval, count its coverage on data it has not seen.

Build-along

Make a band, then make it lie

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You will build a one-number room in Python: it drifts toward 18 C, gets a small random push every hour, and is read by a sensor with 0.4 C of noise. A scalar Kalman filter keeps it in sync; every midnight you forecast 24 hours ahead, carrying the variance forward, and count how many readings land inside the 95 percent band. Then you assume a sensor ten times better than the real one and count again. The figure is the count you should get.

Build the constructed room

Make a scratch folder, open band.py, and start with the world. The room keeps 90 percent of its gap to 18 C each hour, gets a push with sd 0.15 C, and the sensor adds sd 0.4 C. Everything is seeded so your run matches the page.

band.py, part 1import math import random rng = random.Random(9) A, MU = 0.9, 18.0 # each hour the room keeps 90 percent of its gap to 18 C Q_SD, R_SD = 0.15, 0.4 # true hourly disturbance and true sensor noise, in C HOURS, H = 24 * 60, 24 # 60 constructed days; 24-hour forecasts # The constructed world: a true room and the readings of a noisy sensor. x, reads = MU, [] for t in range(HOURS): x = MU + A * (x - MU) + rng.gauss(0, Q_SD) reads.append(x + rng.gauss(0, R_SD))

Filter, forecast and count

One function, given the sensor noise the twin assumes. It filters hour by hour; at each midnight it runs 24 predict steps with no update, carrying the variance forward, and checks each reading against 1.96 times the square root of state variance plus sensor variance.

band.py, part 2def coverage(r_sd): r, q = r_sd ** 2, Q_SD ** 2 m, p = reads[0], r hits = total = 0 width = 0.0 for t in range(1, HOURS): m, p = MU + A * (m - MU), A * A * p + q # predict one hour k = p / (p + r) # update on the reading m, p = m + k * (reads[t] - m), (1 - k) * p if t % 24 == 0 and t + H < HOURS: # each midnight: 24 h ahead fm, fp = m, p for h in range(1, H + 1): fm, fp = MU + A * (fm - MU), A * A * fp + q half = 1.96 * math.sqrt(fp + r) # state variance + sensor variance hits += abs(reads[t + h] - fm) <= half total += 1 width += 2 * half return hits, total, width / total

Count twice and compare

Run the honest noise and a noise ten times too small on the same readings. Save, then run python3 -I band.py from the scratch folder.

band.py, part 3for label, sd in [("honest noise 0.4 C", 0.4), ("assumed 0.04 C", 0.04)]: hits, total, w = coverage(sd) print(f"{label}: {hits} of {total} readings inside, coverage {100 * hits / total:.1f}%, mean width {w:.2f} C")
Outputhonest noise 0.4 C: 1317 of 1392 readings inside, coverage 94.6%, mean width 2.02 C assumed 0.04 C: 1024 of 1392 readings inside, coverage 73.6%, mean width 1.22 C

Read what you made

The honest band lands at 94.6 percent, within a point of nominal. The overconfident one is 40 percent narrower and covers 73.6 percent: the same shape as Kittiwake's 96.8 against 77.4. Note what did not change: the readings, the model, the forecast line. Only the claim about the noise changed, and it changed two things at once, because the filter also trusted each reading more and the band forgot the noise every reading carries.

two edits to try, one at a timeQ_SD = 0.6 # a jumpier room: does the wrong r still hurt as much? H = 6 # shorter forecasts: is the band mostly state or mostly sensor?

The habit to keep. Never ship a band you have not counted. Store every band the twin issues next to the reading that later arrived, and report coverage per asset per week beside the forecast itself. A band that has never been graded is decoration.

Check yourself

Three questions

Told the sensors had 0.01 C of noise, Kittiwake's band shrank from 2.20 C to 1.28 C and covered 77.4 percent of the readings instead of 96.8. Why did the narrower band cover less?

The readings still carry 0.4 C of noise. A band built on 0.01 C omits it, so far more readings fall outside while the label still says 95 percent. The energy estimate was unchanged; only the claim of certainty moved.

In Kittiwake's week-ahead what-if, what is the counterfactual?

Only one schedule runs. The 6,343 kWh baseline exists only because Kittiwake is constructed; in a real building the baseline is itself a model output, which is why savings claims need a stated baseline.

Which uncertainty is NOT inside Kittiwake's 2.20 C band?

The band propagates state uncertainty and adds sensor and controller noise, all for fixed parameters. A band on the saving would need the parameter uncertainty too; tonight it is named, not added.
