Anatomy of a twin

A twin arrives as a slide with four boxes. You will build it as four kinds of data: the asset as a versioned record, the model as a file of parameters, the data link as one row per hour, and a log of the decisions made with it. Kittiwake Hall, a constructed six-storey teaching building, is the case; its facilities team wants a thermal twin of each floor to plan heating. Tonight you read one hour of that twin field by field, with the real engine computing it in the page, and write the four schemas everything else in the track builds on.

Part 0

Kittiwake's twin, as data

Kittiwake Hall (constructed) has six floors, one room sensor and one heat meter per floor, a weather feed, a timetable, and the drawings. The twin's job is to answer "what would this heating schedule use next week, and how sure are you?". Before any model is fitted, someone has to decide what the twin stores, keyed how, and versioned how. Get that wrong and every later session inherits it.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. Four schemas for any twin: an asset record with a configuration version, a model record with parameters and where each came from, an hourly row that separates inputs, measurements and the twin's own predictions, and a decision log. One hour of Kittiwake's twin read field by field: the twin predicts the room, the sensor disagrees, the surprise is multiplied by a gain of about 0.445, and the state moves. And a ten-line Python function for one hour of a two-node floor model that you run yourself.

LiveWhy a twin needs four schemas, not one table4 min▶

A warehouse table has rows of events and columns of attributes. A twin mixes four things with different lifetimes. The asset changes rarely and must be versioned (a retrofit is a new configuration). The model changes when someone recalibrates it, and every prediction must say which model made it. The data link produces a row every hour. Decisions happen weekly and must point at the prediction they relied on. Put them in one table and you lose the ability to ask "which model, of which version of the building, said this?".

Real world

The AIAA and AIA (2020) position paper describes a twin following its asset through As Designed, As Built, As Used and As Maintained, and names models, and data from both the models and the physical asset, as critical elements. Those phases are versions of the asset record; the models and their outputs are separate records that must point at them.

Self-studyWords for the data side3 min▶
  • Configuration version. An id for the asset as it physically is; it changes with every logged physical change.
  • Input. Something the twin is given and does not predict: weather, sun, the heat meter, the timetable.
  • Measurement. Something the twin predicts and then checks: here, the room sensor.
  • Prior and posterior. The twin's estimate before and after it sees the hour's measurement.
  • Innovation. Measurement minus prior: the surprise. Practitioner session 5 watches it for drift.
  • Gain. The share of the surprise the twin accepts. Practitioner session 4 derives it.
Part 1 · covers the asset record and the model record

The asset is a record with versions; the model is a file with parameters

15 min live

The asset record says which physical thing this is and which configuration it is in. The model record says which equations, which parameter values, and where each value came from. The figure draws both for floor 4 and the link between them: a model is fitted to one configuration, and stops being trustworthy the day the configuration changes.

LiveThe asset record5 min▶

Key it on the physical thing (floor KH-F4), not on a sensor: sensors are replaced, floors are not. Give it a configuration version that increments on every logged physical change, and a change log with date, what changed, who logged it. The sensors and meters attached to it are their own records with install dates. The test: given any hour in the past, can you say which configuration the floor was in? If not, practitioner session 6 is your next job.

LiveThe model record5 min▶

A model record names the structure (here two thermal nodes per floor: room air and building mass), a version, and every parameter with its value and its provenance: from the drawings, from calibration on which data, or assumed. In Kittiwake's bench, calibration recovers each floor's heat-loss multiplier within 0.08 but cannot pin down the mass from one week (floor 4: 0.90 found against 0.88 true; floor 3: 1.68 found against 0.89 true). A provenance field that says "calibrated, weakly identified" is the honest record of that. Practitioner session 3 shows why.

Self-studyWhere this sits in the standards3 min▶

ISO 23247-1:2021 sets out a digital twin framework for manufacturing and, from its published contents, frames the twin around "observable manufacturing elements" (reported: the standard is paywalled and its clauses were not read here). The National Academies (2024) report asks for twins to be defined at a fidelity that is fit for purpose. Neither prescribes a schema; both assume you can say what is being observed and what the model claims about it.

Part 2 · covers the hourly row and one hour of the twin

One hour of the twin, field by field

10 min live

Each hour the twin does three things in order: it predicts the room from the last state and the hour's inputs, it reads the sensor, and it corrects its state by a share of the surprise. The row it writes must keep these apart, because practitioner sessions 4, 5 and 7 each need a different column.

LiveReading the row5 min▶

Inputs carry the previous hour. The heat meter's 20.00 kW is what went in between 08:00 and 09:00. Mixing up which hour an input belongs to is the most common bug in a twin's data link, and it does not throw an error: it just makes the model a little worse everywhere.

The prior is the model's claim. 17.42 C from physics and inputs alone. The sensor says 16.44. The surprise, -0.99 C, is the single most useful number the twin produces.

The posterior is a compromise. With a gain of 0.445 the twin moves 44.5 percent of the way toward the reading: 16.99 C. In this hour the truth (17.14 C) was between them. Step through the hours: sometimes the reading is closer, sometimes the prior. On average the blend wins (0.218 C against the sensors' 0.404 C over the week, practitioner session 10).

Self-studyWhy the mass is in the state but not in the row3 min▶

The twin also carries the temperature of the building's mass, which no sensor reads. The update moves it too, through how the room and the mass have moved together in the model. Store it in the twin's state table, with the model version that produced it, but never in the measurement columns: it is an estimate, not an observation.

Part 3 · covers the data link and the decision log

The link and the log

5 min live

The data link is everything that turns the building into hourly rows: feeds, clocks, gap-filling, the meter's units. The decision log closes the loop: what was decided, on which prediction, from which model version. The figure shows the four records and the keys that join them.

LiveWhat belongs to the data link, and what does not3 min▶

Clocks, resampling, gaps, sensor triage and missing readings are their own discipline and live in learn IoT data. For a twin, the link has one extra duty: tell the twin when a reading is missing rather than filling it silently, so the twin can predict through the gap and not mistake an interpolation for evidence.

Self-studyWhy the decision log is part of the twin2 min▶

The National Academies (2024) definition ends with "informs decisions that realize value". Without a log you cannot check whether the twin's advice was any good, and you cannot tell which past decisions to revisit when a model version turns out to be wrong. Practitioner session 8 builds governance on this log.

Build-along

Write Kittiwake's four schemas and run one hour

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You will write the four schemas for Kittiwake's twin, then run one hour of a floor model in Python and see what heat does in an hour. The figure is the model you will code: two temperatures, three heat paths, one input.

Write the asset record

One record per floor, keyed on the floor, with a configuration version and a change log. Copy and adapt.

asset.yamlfloor_id: KH-F4 config_version: 3 as_designed: drawings, 6 storeys, end floors lose 1.3x change_log: - version: 3 date: (when) change: radiator swapped logged_by: (who) attached: [T-F4-01, H-F4]

Write the model record

Every parameter with value and provenance. Mark the weakly identified one.

model.yamlmodel_version: 2 structure: 2-node RC per floor (room air, mass) fitted_to: {floor_id: KH-F4, config_version: 3, data: week 1} params: Ci: {value: 6.0, unit: kWh/K, from: drawings} kU: {value: 1.42, from: calibration week 1} kC: {value: 0.90, from: calibration week 1, note: weakly identified} sensor_sd: {value: 0.4, unit: C, from: assumed}

Write the hourly row and the decision log

Keep inputs, the reading, and the twin's own numbers in separate, named columns, each row carrying both versions.

hourly_row and decision_loghourly_row: floor_id, hour, to_c, sun_kw, heat_kw, timetable_kw, reading_c, prior_c, surprise_c, gain, posterior_c, config_version, model_version decision_log: decision_id, date, decision, owner, prediction_relied_on, band, model_version

Run one hour of the floor model

Save as step.py in a scratch folder and run python3 step.py. These are the lines the engine uses, without the filter.

step.py# One hour of a 2-node RC floor model, explicit Euler in 4 quarter-hour steps. def step(ti, tm, to, q, Ci=6.0, Cm=20.0, Rim=0.5, Rio=10.0, Rmo=4.0): for _ in range(4): dti = ((tm - ti) / Rim + (to - ti) / Rio + q) / Ci dtm = ((ti - tm) / Rim + (to - tm) / Rmo) / Cm ti, tm = ti + 0.25 * dti, tm + 0.25 * dtm return ti, tm # Room 20 C, mass 19 C, 5 C outside, 6 kW of heat into the room for one hour. ti, tm = step(20.0, 19.0, 5.0, 6.0) print(f"room {ti:.2f} C, mass {tm:.2f} C") # The same hour with no heat at all. ti0, tm0 = step(20.0, 19.0, 5.0, 0.0) print(f"no heat: room {ti0:.2f} C, mass {tm0:.2f} C")
Outputroom 20.36 C, mass 18.94 C no heat: room 19.48 C, mass 18.91 C

Read what an hour does

Six kilowatts for an hour moved the room by less than a degree, and the mass barely moved. Change kU by dividing Rio and Rmo by 1.4 and run again: the floor you actually have, not the one you drew. Leader session 3 is about that difference.

The habit to keep. Every number a twin writes carries two versions: the configuration of the asset and the version of the model. Without them, no prediction can be checked later and no decision can be traced.

Check yourself

Three questions

What should an asset record for one of Kittiwake's floors be keyed on?

Sensors are replaced and models are refitted; the floor stays. The configuration version is how a later reader knows which building a model was fitted to.

At 09:00 the twin predicted 17.42 C, the sensor read 16.44 C and the gain is 0.445 (constructed). What is the posterior?

The twin moves 44.5 percent of the way toward the reading. In this hour the true room (17.14 C) was between the two.

Why must the hourly row store the prior and the surprise, not only the posterior?

After correction the posterior looks fine even when the model is wrong. Practitioner session 5 detects an unlogged retrofit from the surprises alone.

Cheat sheet

Four records. Asset, model, hourly row, decision log.
Asset key. The physical thing plus a configuration version.
Model record. Structure, version, every parameter with value and provenance.
Hourly row. Inputs, reading, prior, surprise, gain, posterior, both versions.
Inputs lag. The heat meter value belongs to the hour before the reading.
The update. Posterior = prior + gain x (reading - prior).
Estimates are not observations. The mass temperature lives in the state table, never in measurements.
Constructed. Kittiwake's numbers are generated; your numbers will differ.
