When the model drifts and when the asset does

A twin and its building can fall out of step in two ways. The model can be wrong from the start, or the building can change under it. Kittiwake Hall, a constructed six-storey teaching building, gets an insulation retrofit on floor 4 that nobody logs. The filter keeps tracking the room, so the tracking error barely moves. The hourly surprises do move, and tonight you learn to read them: a week-against-week test on the innovations, run live in the page, then the triage that turns a flag into a cause.

Part 0

The twin looks fine, and it is wrong

In practitioner session 4 the twin learned to correct itself every hour. That is the good news and the trap: a filter that corrects itself will also quietly correct away the evidence that the building changed. Kittiwake Hall (constructed) runs its calibrated twin through week 2, and at the start of that week floor 4 is insulated and nobody tells the twin. Its tracking error on floor 4 is 0.399 C: worse than the building's 0.218 C, but nobody's alarm goes off at 0.4 C. The surprises, the readings minus the predictions, carry the real story.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A clear line between model drift (the twin was wrong from day one or decays) and asset drift (the building changed at a moment). A week-against-week test on each floor's innovations that flags floor 4 alone in Kittiwake's bench (z 11.44 against a threshold of 4). A three-branch triage for any flag: asset, sensor, model. And a twenty-line Python script with a CUSUM that you run yourself and watch climb after a step.

LiveWhy "the twin still tracks" is not "the twin is right"3 min▶

Tracking error asks: is the twin's corrected state close to the room? A filter makes that true by design, because it pulls its state toward every reading. The question a twin owner needs is different: does the model still predict the room before it sees the reading? That lives in the innovation column of the hourly row from practitioner session 1, and only there. If you stored only posteriors, the evidence is gone.

Real world

The National Academies (2024) report on digital twins concludes that verification, validation and uncertainty quantification must be a continual process for a twin, one that adapts to changes in the physical counterpart. A twin validated once and then left running is exactly the case this session is about: the building moved on, the validation did not.

Self-studyWords for drift3 min▶
  • Innovation. Reading minus the twin's prediction for that hour: the surprise, before any correction.
  • Model drift. The model disagrees with the asset because the model is wrong: a wrong parameter, a missing heat path, a bias present from day one or growing slowly.
  • Asset drift. The model disagrees because the asset changed at a moment: a retrofit, a swapped radiator, a moved sensor.
  • Shift test. Compare the mean surprise in two windows and ask whether the difference is bigger than noise explains.
  • Welch z. The difference in means divided by its standard error, allowing each window its own spread.
  • CUSUM. A running sum of surprises above an allowance that sounds an alarm when it crosses a threshold. Named here; detectors are taught in learn anomaly detection.
Part 1 · covers model drift and asset drift

Two kinds of drift, two shapes of surprise

8 min live

The two kinds of drift leave different fingerprints in the surprises. A model that was wrong from the start shows a bias from day one. A building that changed shows a step at a date. The figure draws both shapes; the step is the one with a date you can go and look up.

LiveModel drift: the twin was wrong before anything happened4 min▶

Run the design-spec twin of Kittiwake (practitioner session 3) through the filter and its surprises are biased from the first hour: the drawings say the floors lose less heat than they do, so the model keeps predicting a warmer room than the sensor finds. Nothing changed; the model was simply never right. The same signature appears when a parameter slowly decays, such as a heat-loss value that ages with the building: a slow ramp rather than a step. The fix is on the model side: recalibrate, and record a new model version.

LiveAsset drift: the building changed at a moment4 min▶

A retrofit, a swapped radiator, new windows, a sensor moved from the wall to a desk: each is a physical change with a date. Before it, the calibrated model was right; after it, the model describes a building that no longer exists. In Kittiwake's bench the retrofit cuts floor 4's heat loss to 0.6 times what it was, at the start of week 2, and the twin is not told. The surprises step up and stay up. The fix is on the asset side first: log the change as a new configuration, then refit the model to it.

Self-studyWhy the line between them is blurry in practice3 min▶

A model calibrated on one week can look fine and still be wrong for a season it never saw: that is model drift that only appears when conditions change, and it can look like a step on the day the weather turns. Gradual asset changes (filters clogging, seals ageing) produce ramps that look like model decay. The shapes are a first guess, never a verdict. The AIAA and AIA (2020) position paper names an "As Maintained" phase in a twin's life: the asset keeps changing after it is built, and the twin has to be told.

Part 2 · covers the innovation shift test

The innovation test: week against week, floor by floor

8 min live

Kittiwake's bench runs one simple test on every floor. Take the mean surprise in week 1 (hours 25 to 167, after a day of warm-up) and in week 2 (hours 168 to 336). Divide the difference by its standard error, with each week allowed its own spread (Welch's z). Flag the floor when the size of z is above 4. The figure shows why the test looks at the surprises and not at the tracking error.

Synthetic and constructed: Kittiwake, its weather, readings and the retrofit are generated. The retrofit, when on, cuts floor 4's heat loss to 0.6 times at the start of week 2 and is not logged.

LiveReading the table4 min▶

Floor 4 stands alone. At the canon settings its mean surprise is +0.015 C in week 1 and +0.638 C in week 2, z 11.44. Every other floor stays below 0.7 in size. Turn the retrofit off and no floor is flagged: z runs from -0.65 to 0.68 across the six floors.

The tracking error barely notices. Floor 4's tracking error with the retrofit is 0.399 C, against 0.218 C for the whole building without it. Neither number would make anyone look twice. The filter takes about 0.445 of every surprise into its state, practitioner session 4's gain, so it keeps following the room while the model underneath is describing a floor that no longer exists.

Change the seed. Seeds 1, 7 and 42 give a break z of 8.7, 11.6 and 11.9, always on floor 4 and only there. Your numbers will differ by seed; the shape holds.

LiveWhat the wrong state costs2 min▶

A good-looking state hides a bad plan. A week-ahead heating plan runs the model forward without readings, so it uses the old parameters. In the bench, floor 4's energy estimate for the setback week is 63.1 percent too high and the whole building's 8.9 percent too high (5,153 against 4,732 kWh). The twin says the setback week will cost far more heat on floor 4 than it does. Energy figures here are constructed and are not investment or financial advice.

Self-studyWhy a threshold of 4 and two whole weeks3 min▶

Six floors are tested at once, so a loose threshold would flag a clean floor now and then by chance alone; 4 is strict enough that the clean runs never get close (the largest clean z across the four seeds is under 1.5 in size). Hourly surprises are also correlated from one hour to the next, which makes a naive z look more certain than it is: one more reason for a strict threshold. Comparing whole weeks is slow: you learn about a Monday change the following Sunday. Faster detectors trade speed against false alarms; that trade is the subject of learn anomaly detection. Kalman (1960) set filtering in a state-space form; the surprises the filter computes each hour are a by-product it already has, so watching them costs nothing extra.

Part 3 · covers turning a flag into a cause

From a flag to a cause

4 min live

A flag says one thing: the model and the asset disagree on this floor, starting about here. It does not say why. Three things can have changed, and each has its own evidence. The figure is the triage order most twin owners settle on: check the cheapest record first.

LiveThree checks, in order3 min▶

The change log. Was anything done to floor 4 around the start of week 2? If the log says yes, you have a cause and a new configuration version to fit to. If the log says no, that is not proof nothing happened: in Kittiwake's bench the retrofit was real and the log was silent. Practitioner session 6 treats that gap as a data problem.

The sensor. A sensor moved closer to a radiator, swapped for a new one with a different offset, or a clock that slipped an hour all produce a step in the surprises with nothing wrong in the building. Sensor health has its own discipline in learn IoT data.

The model version. Did a new model version go live that week? Every hourly row carries its model version (practitioner session 1), so this is a query, not a meeting. A flag that starts the hour a new version deployed points at the version.

Self-studyWhat a flag does not tell you3 min▶
  • Not the cause. All three branches produce the same step.
  • Not the exact hour. A week-against-week test says "somewhere this week"; finding the hour takes a change-point method or a CUSUM, as in the build-along.
  • Not the size of the physical change. A mean surprise of +0.638 C is not "heat loss x 0.6"; recovering that takes a recalibration on the new week.
  • Not whether to act. That belongs to the twin's owner and its governance, practitioner session 8.

Automating root cause, deciding which branch it was without a person, is out of scope here: it needs the change log, the sensor history and the model registry joined, and judgement about which to trust.

Build-along

Catch a step in the surprises with a week test and a CUSUM

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You will simulate two weeks of hourly surprises for one floor, put a step of +0.6 C in at hour 168, and catch it twice: once with the week-against-week Welch z from Part 2, and once with a one-sided CUSUM that names the hour. Standard library only. The figure is the CUSUM from this exact script around the step.

Make a scratch folder and the surprises

Make an empty folder, save the script below as drift.py in it, and read the first block. 336 hourly surprises with a standard deviation of 0.3 C stand in for one floor's innovations. From hour 168 every surprise gets +0.6 C: the floor changed and the twin was not told.

drift.py, part 1# Innovations for one floor, two weeks hourly, with a step at the start of week 2. import random, statistics, math rng = random.Random(11) innov = [rng.gauss(0.0, 0.3) for _ in range(336)] # surprises, sd 0.3 C for t in range(168, 336): innov[t] += 0.6 # the asset changed at hour 168

Add the week-against-week test

The same Welch z as Kittiwake's bench: the difference of the two weekly means over its standard error, each week with its own variance.

drift.py, part 2w1, w2 = innov[:168], innov[168:] m1, m2 = statistics.mean(w1), statistics.mean(w2) z = (m2 - m1) / math.sqrt(statistics.variance(w1) / len(w1) + statistics.variance(w2) / len(w2)) print(f"week 1 mean {m1:+.3f} C, week 2 mean {m2:+.3f} C, Welch z {z:.2f}")

Add a one-sided CUSUM

Each hour, add the surprise minus an allowance k (here half the step you care about), never let the sum go below 0, and sound the alarm when it passes h. Record the path so you can look at it.

drift.py, part 3# One-sided CUSUM: add each surprise minus an allowance k, never go below 0, alarm above h. k, h, s, alarm, path = 0.3, 1.5, 0.0, None, [] for t, v in enumerate(innov): s = max(0.0, s + v - k) path.append(s) if s > h and alarm is None: alarm = t print(f"highest CUSUM in week 1: {max(path[:168]):.2f} (threshold {h})") print(f"CUSUM alarm at hour {alarm}, {alarm - 168} hours after the change") print("CUSUM at hours 168 to 180:", " ".join(f"{x:.2f}" for x in path[168:181]))

Run it and read the output

From the scratch folder, run python3 -I drift.py. This is the output it printed when we ran it:

Outputweek 1 mean +0.059 C, week 2 mean +0.570 C, Welch z 15.21 highest CUSUM in week 1: 0.78 (threshold 1.5) CUSUM alarm at hour 174, 6 hours after the change CUSUM at hours 168 to 180: 0.26 0.10 0.57 0.23 0.26 0.93 1.72 1.67 1.51 2.07 2.62 2.63 2.72

Break it on purpose

Set h to 0.5 and run again: the alarm fires in week 1, before anything changed. Then set the step to +0.2 instead of +0.6 and watch how much later the alarm comes, or whether it comes at all. That trade, earlier alarms against false ones, is what detector design is about; take it further in learn anomaly detection.

two edits to try, one at a timek, h, s, alarm, path = 0.3, 0.5, 0.0, None, [] # a jumpier alarm innov[t] += 0.2 # a smaller change

The habit to keep. Store every surprise, not just the corrected state, and run a shift test on them per asset, every week. A twin whose surprises are never read will absorb a changed building without a word.

Check yourself

Three questions

After the unlogged retrofit, floor 4's tracking error is only 0.399 C (constructed). Why does that number hide the change?

The update moves the state about 0.445 of the way to each reading. The state stays close; the predictions do not, and the mean surprise jumps from +0.015 to +0.638 C.

The surprises on a floor have been about +0.5 C since the first hour of week 1 and are the same in week 2. Which kind of drift does that shape suggest first?

A bias present from day one has no date to look up; it points at the model. A step on a date points at the building. Treat both as a first guess and check the records.

Floor 4 is flagged with z 11.44. What does the flag not tell you?

The flag says the model and the asset disagree on floor 4, this week. The cause takes the triage in Part 3.
