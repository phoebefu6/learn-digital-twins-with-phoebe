Keeping state in sync: the Kalman filter by hand

A calibrated model still drifts away from the building hour by hour, because the weather, the people and the sensor all carry noise the model never sees. The Kalman filter is how a twin stays in sync: predict, read the sensor, correct by a share of the surprise. Tonight you work it by hand on one temperature, watch the gain settle to a number you can derive on paper, and see how Kittiwake Hall's twin (a constructed six-storey teaching building) corrects a building mass that no sensor ever reads.

Part 0

Two witnesses, both a little wrong

Every hour, Kittiwake's twin (constructed) has two opinions about each floor's room temperature. The model says what physics and the inputs predict. The sensor says what it read, with noise of about 0.4 C. Neither is the truth. The Kalman filter is the disciplined way to weigh them, and the weight it lands on, the gain, is the most honest number a twin publishes about itself.

★ What you walk out with tonight. The four lines of a scalar Kalman filter, written and run by you in standard-library Python. The closed form for where the gain settles on a random walk, checked against your own run: 0.5198 for q = 0.09 and r = 0.16. A clear picture of what goes wrong when the sensor noise is assumed too small: the gain climbs to 0.999 and the filter copies the noise. And the one idea that makes a two-state twin work: the off-diagonal term of the covariance carries a correction to a state nobody measures.

LiveWhy a twin needs a filter at all3 min▶

Run a calibrated model open loop and its errors pile up: a cold morning it did not foresee, a full lecture hall, a door left open. Copy the sensor instead and you inherit every bit of its noise. In Kittiwake's bench (constructed, seed 1124) the calibrated model run open loop is 0.482 C from the true room in week 2, the sensors alone are 0.404 C off, and the filtered twin is 0.218 C off: better than either witness on its own.

Real world

The National Academies (2024) report puts this loop at the centre of what a twin is: "The bidirectional interaction between the virtual and the physical is central to the digital twin." Its Conclusion 2-1 names the mechanism as dynamic data-driven model updating. A Kalman filter is the oldest and plainest form of that updating.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • State, x. What the twin believes right now: here, the room temperature (and later, the mass temperature).
  • Variance, P. How unsure the twin is about its state, in degrees squared.
  • Process variance, q. How much the real state can wander in one step that the model cannot foresee.
  • Sensor variance, r. How noisy one reading is. A sensor with sd 0.4 C has r = 0.16.
  • Innovation, or surprise. Reading minus prediction. Practitioner session 5 watches it for drift.
  • Gain, K. The share of the surprise the twin accepts, between 0 and 1.
Part 1 · covers the predict and update steps, and the gain

Predict, read, correct

8 min live

Start with the smallest possible twin: one room, one number. Treat the room temperature as a random walk: each step it wanders by an amount with variance q that nothing predicts. The sensor reads it with noise of variance r. Each cycle the filter does two things. Predict: keep the estimate, add q to the variance. Update: compute the gain K = P / (P + r), move the estimate by K times the surprise, and multiply the variance by (1 - K).

LiveThe two steps, in words and in symbols4 min▶

Predict. For a random walk the best guess for the next step is the current estimate, x stays. But the room may have wandered, so the variance grows: P becomes P + q. Uncertainty always grows between readings.

Update. The reading y arrives. The surprise is y - x. The gain K = P / (P + r) compares how unsure the twin is with how unsure the sensor is. The estimate moves by K times the surprise, and the variance shrinks to (1 - K) times P. Uncertainty always shrinks when evidence arrives.

In the figure the two witnesses are equally unsure, so K = 0.5 and the posterior sits halfway. Make the sensor noisier and K drops toward 0: the twin barely moves. Make the twin less sure and K rises toward 1: the twin follows the reading.

LiveWhere the method comes from2 min▶

Kalman (1960), "A New Approach to Linear Filtering and Prediction Problems", Journal of Basic Engineering 82(1): 35-45, recast filtering in state-space form, with the error covariance following its own recursion alongside the estimate. That second recursion is the part people skip and the part that matters: the filter does not just track the state, it tracks how sure it is. The predict and update form taught here is the standard modern presentation of that idea.

Real world

The recursion is cheap: a handful of multiplications per reading, no history kept. That is what makes it practical for a twin that runs for years: it only ever carries the current state and its covariance forward.

Self-studyWhy the posterior is narrower than both3 min▶

Multiply two bell curves and you get a third bell curve whose precision (one over the variance) is the sum of the two precisions. Prior precision 1 / 0.16 plus reading precision 1 / 0.16 gives 2 / 0.16, so the posterior variance is 0.08. The update formula (1 - K) P is the same arithmetic written in a form that never divides by a tiny variance. Two independent witnesses always leave you surer than either one, provided they really are independent and you have their noise right.

Part 2 · covers the steady-state gain and the closed form

Where the gain settles

7 min live

Run the two steps over and over with fixed q and r and something useful happens: the variance and the gain stop changing, whatever the readings say. For a random walk you can solve for that resting point on paper. The variance just before an update settles at P = (q + sqrt(q² + 4qr)) / 2, and the gain at K = P / (P + r). With q = 0.09 and r = 0.16 that gives K = 0.5198, and the engine behind this page checks exactly that number every time it loads.

LiveReading the widget4 min▶

At the defaults the gain is 0.8720 after step 1 (the twin started unsure, so it trusts the first reading heavily), 0.5206 by step 5, and 0.5198 from step 10 on, equal to the closed form. The start is forgotten fast.

Raise r to 0.64, a noisy sensor: the gain settles at 0.3112. The twin leans on its model and takes only a third of each surprise.

Lower r to 0.01: the gain climbs to 0.9083 and the twin mostly follows the reading. Now try q = 0.01 with r = 0.04 and q = 0.04 with r = 0.16: both give 0.3904. The gain only knows the ratio of q to r.

LiveThe anti-lever: tell the filter the sensor is perfect3 min▶

Practitioner session 10 has a button for this. Assume a sensor sd of 0.01 C when the true noise is 0.4 C, and Kittiwake's filter sets its gain to 0.999 (constructed, seed 1124). The twin now copies every reading, noise included: its tracking error is 0.403 C against the sensors' own 0.404 C. All the work the model was doing is thrown away. Nothing errors, nothing looks broken; the twin simply becomes an expensive copy of the sensor, and its band of uncertainty shrinks to a width it cannot honour (practitioner session 7).

Self-studyDeriving the closed form on paper4 min▶

Call P the variance just before an update. After the update it is (1 - K) P = P r / (P + r). The next predict adds q. At the resting point that brings you back to P:

P = P r / (P + r) + q

Multiply through by (P + r) and tidy: P² - q P - q r = 0. Take the positive root: P = (q + sqrt(q² + 4qr)) / 2. With q = 0.09 and r = 0.16 this is 0.1732, and K = 0.1732 / (0.1732 + 0.16) = 0.5198. Divide both q and r by the same number and P scales with them while K does not: the ratio is all that matters.

Part 3 · covers two states, the covariance, Q and R

Two states, one sensor

5 min live

Kittiwake's twin (constructed) carries two temperatures per floor: the room air and the building mass. Only the room has a sensor. Yet every hour the mass estimate moves too. The reason sits in the 2x2 covariance: its off-diagonal term records that the room and the mass have been moving together in the model, and the update uses it to pass a share of the room's surprise on to the mass.

LiveWhat changes from one state to two2 min▶

The state becomes a pair [room, mass] and P becomes a 2x2 matrix. Predict runs the floor model from practitioner session 1 forward an hour and pushes the covariance through the same physics, then adds Q. Update looks exactly like the scalar case for the room, with S = P room + r, and adds a second gain for the mass: P room-mass / S. In Kittiwake's bench the gain on the room reading averages 0.445 to 0.448 per floor over week 2, and the filtered twin tracks the true room to 0.218 C, against 0.404 C for the sensors alone and 0.482 C for the calibrated model run open loop (constructed, seed 1124).

The bench uses the same q and r as your scalar run, (0.3 C)² for the room and (0.4 C)² for the sensor, yet settles lower than 0.5198. The physics is why: each hour the model pulls the room toward the mass, so the room's uncertainty carried over from the last hour shrinks before q is added, and the twin trusts its prediction a little more.

Real world

Bacher and Madsen (2011), in Energy and Buildings 43(7), describe building heat dynamics as grey-box stochastic differential equations: RC physics with noise terms on the states (record read, method reported). That shape, physics plus declared noise per state, is exactly what a Kalman filter needs as input.

LiveQ is model humility; R must be the real sensor2 min▶
  • Q says how wrong the model may be each step. Kittiwake's bench declares q_room = 0.3 and q_mass = 0.05 per hour (as standard deviations, squared on the diagonal of Q). Too small a Q and the twin grows stubborn: it discounts real surprises. Too large and it chases noise. Q is a modelling choice; declare it in the model record, next to the parameters.
  • R is not a choice, it is a fact about the sensor. Set it from the sensor's measured noise. Set it too small and you get the anti-lever: gain 0.999, a twin that copies the noise. How to measure a sensor's noise, and how clocks and gaps distort it, lives in learn IoT data.
Self-studyWhen the model is not linear2 min▶

Kittiwake's floor model is linear in its two temperatures, so the plain filter is exact for it. Many real assets are not. The extended, unscented and ensemble Kalman filters, and particle filters, are the families built for that case; they are named here, not taught. The National Academies (2024) treat data assimilation as part of a twin's verification, validation and uncertainty quantification, which they want run as a continual process rather than once at commissioning.

Build-along

Write a scalar Kalman filter and break it on purpose

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You will simulate a room temperature that wanders, read it through a noisy sensor, filter it with the four lines from Part 1, check the gain against the closed form, and then lie to the filter about the sensor and watch it copy the noise. Standard-library Python only. The figure is the loop you will write.

Simulate a wandering room and a noisy sensor

Make a scratch folder and start a file called kalman1d.py. The truth is a random walk from 20 C with step variance q = 0.09; the sensor adds noise with variance r = 0.16. The seed is fixed at 3 so the run is reproducible; the readings are constructed.

kalman1d.py, part 1# A scalar Kalman filter on a simulated room temperature (a random walk). import math import random def simulate(q, r, n=200, seed=3): rng = random.Random(seed) truth, readings, t = [], [], 20.0 for _ in range(n): t += rng.gauss(0.0, math.sqrt(q)) # the room wanders truth.append(t) readings.append(t + rng.gauss(0.0, math.sqrt(r))) # the sensor is noisy return truth, readings

Write the filter and check the gain

The loop is the four boxes in the figure. Append it, run 200 steps, and compare the final gain with the closed form from Part 2.

kalman1d.py, part 2def kalman(readings, q, r): x, p = readings[0], r # start at the first reading, as unsure as the sensor estimates, gain = [x], 0.0 for y in readings[1:]: p = p + q # predict: a random walk keeps x, adds q to the variance gain = p / (p + r) # gain: how much of the surprise to accept x = x + gain * (y - x) # update: move by gain times the surprise p = (1 - gain) * p # the variance shrinks estimates.append(x) return estimates, gain def rmse(a, b): return math.sqrt(sum((u - v) ** 2 for u, v in zip(a, b)) / len(a)) q, r = 0.09, 0.16 truth, readings = simulate(q, r) est, gain = kalman(readings, q, r) pp = (q + math.sqrt(q * q + 4 * q * r)) / 2 print(f"final gain {gain:.4f}, closed form {pp / (pp + r):.4f}")

Score raw readings against the filter

Because the world is simulated you know the truth, which a real building never gives you. Measure both witnesses against it.

kalman1d.py, part 3print(f"RMSE raw readings {rmse(readings, truth):.3f} C, filtered {rmse(est, truth):.3f} C")

Lie to the filter about the sensor

Same readings, but tell the filter r = 0.0001, a near-perfect sensor, while the true noise is still 0.16. Then run the whole file with python3 kalman1d.py.

kalman1d.py, part 4est2, gain2 = kalman(readings, q, 0.0001) # same data, sensor assumed almost perfect print(f"r assumed 0.0001: gain {gain2:.4f}, filtered RMSE {rmse(est2, truth):.3f} C")
Outputfinal gain 0.5198, closed form 0.5198 RMSE raw readings 0.398 C, filtered 0.318 C r assumed 0.0001: gain 0.9989, filtered RMSE 0.398 C

Read what you just did

The honest filter settled on exactly the closed-form gain and cut the error from 0.398 C to 0.318 C on this constructed walk. The lying filter set its gain to 0.9989 and its error went straight back to the sensor's 0.398 C: it copied the noise. That is the same anti-lever Kittiwake's bench shows at building scale (0.403 C against 0.404 C). Change the seed and your numbers will differ; the gap between honest and lying r will not.

The habit to keep. Write r down as a measured fact about the sensor, with where it came from, and q down as a declared modelling choice. A filter is only as honest as those two numbers, and neither one ever throws an error when it is wrong.

Check yourself

Three questions

Kittiwake's twin has a gain of about 0.445 on the room reading (constructed). What does that number mean?

The gain is the share of the surprise the twin accepts. It comes from comparing the twin's own uncertainty with the sensor's: K = P / (P + r).

The true sensor noise is 0.4 C, but the filter is told 0.01 C. What happens?

Nothing errors. The twin silently throws away the model and becomes a copy of the sensor; in the bench its band also stops covering the readings it should (practitioner session 7).

No sensor reads Kittiwake's building mass. Why does its estimate still get corrected every hour?

The mass gain is P room-mass / S. With a zero cross term the mass would never learn anything from the sensor.

Cheat sheet

Predict. x by the model; P = P + q. Uncertainty grows between readings.
Gain. K = P / (P + r): the share of the surprise to accept.
Update. x = x + K (y - x); P = (1 - K) P.
Steady state. P = (q + sqrt(q² + 4qr)) / 2; K = 0.5198 at q 0.09, r 0.16.
Ratio only. The gain depends on q / r, never on the readings.
r too small. Gain near 1, the twin copies the noise (constructed: 0.403 C vs 0.404 C).
Two states. The cross term in P corrects the unmeasured mass.
Q and R. Q is declared humility; R is a measured fact. Record both.
