Why the warehouse playbook breaks here

A data team that has shipped warehouses, dashboards and churn models arrives at a twin project with a playbook that has worked for years. Five of its habits quietly stop being true: where the data comes from, what a key means, what counts as the right answer, whether the data stands still, and what a wrong number costs. Tonight takes them one mechanism at a time on Kittiwake Hall, the constructed six-storey teaching building every session uses; every Kittiwake number is generated in the page, not observed. Nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

The analyst who came from the warehouse

Kittiwake Hall is constructed. The analyst who picked up its twin ticket in session 1 has now run it for two weeks, and she keeps reaching for habits that served her in a retail warehouse: trust the system of record, join on the key, score against the label, treat the data as history, fix a wrong number in the next refresh. Each habit is sound where it came from. Each one rests on an assumption a building does not honour. The fix is not to throw the playbook away (leader session 6 sorts what carries over); it is to know exactly which page fails, and what check takes its place.

LiveWhy a twin is a different kind of data project3 min▶

A warehouse sits downstream of software. Every order, refund and stock count was written by a system first, and the warehouse copies those writes. If a value is wrong, the source system is wrong, and someone can fix it there.

A twin sits downstream of a physical thing. Nobody writes the building. It loses heat at whatever rate its walls, windows and radiators allow, today, and the only record of that is what the sensors and meters happen to catch. That single difference, upstream is matter rather than software, is where all five mechanisms come from.

Real world

The AIAA and AIA (2020) position paper names three essential elements of a twin, a virtual representation, a physical realization and a transfer of data between them, and draws the consequence plainly: to have a digital twin requires a physical asset. It then follows that asset through phases called As Designed, As Built, As Used and As Maintained. A warehouse table has no "As Maintained"; a building is never anything else.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • System of record. The software that owns a fact, such as an order system. Warehouses trust it because every change passes through it.
  • Configuration. What the asset physically is right now: which radiators, which insulation, which windows. It changes when facilities change it.
  • Asset record and model record. Two separate things: the record of the floor as built and maintained, and the record of the model fitted to it. Practitioner session 1 draws them apart.
  • Ground truth. In a supervised model, the label you score against. In a building, the true room temperature, which no instrument reads exactly.
  • Innovation. The surprise: what the sensor read minus what the twin predicted. With no ground truth, the surprises are the main evidence a twin has.
  • Coverage. How often reality fell inside the twin's stated band. A 95 percent band should hold the reading about 95 percent of the time.
  • Constructed. Kittiwake Hall and every number about it are invented so the truth is knowable. The research and position papers are real and cited.
Part 1 · covers the source and the identity

The source changes on its own, and a key is not an identity

12 min live

The first two mechanisms are about what the data is attached to. In a warehouse, a change to the business is a write to a system, and a row's key means the same thing forever. In a twin, the asset changes by being worked on, no system hears about it, and "floor 4" names a different physical thing after each retrofit. Both panels below draw where the warehouse assumption sits and where the building breaks it.

LiveMechanism 1 · the source is a physical asset that changes on its own5 min▶

The warehouse habit. Trust the system of record. If the warehouse disagrees with it, the warehouse is wrong; if the system of record is wrong, someone corrects it there and the next load picks it up.

What breaks. A building has no system of record for its own physics. A contractor insulates a floor, a radiator is swapped for a larger one, a window is replaced, and the heat loss changes that afternoon. Nothing in the data pipeline is notified. The sensors keep reporting; the model keeps predicting with its old parameters; the two slowly disagree.

On Kittiwake's bench (constructed). The break scenario insulates floor 4 at the start of week 2 without logging it. The room estimate still looks fine, because the hourly correction follows the sensor. But floor 4's heating plan goes 63.1 percent wrong, and the whole building's plan 8.9 percent wrong, from one unrecorded job on one floor.

The check that replaces the habit. A change log owned by facilities, treated as a data source, and the twin's surprises watched against it. When floor 4's surprises jump, the first question is "what did someone do to floor 4?", and the log should already hold the answer. Practitioner session 6 builds that log.

LiveMechanism 2 · identity is a configuration version, not a row key4 min▶

The warehouse habit. A stable key means the same entity forever. Order 10482 is order 10482 in every table, and joins on it are safe.

What breaks. "Floor 4" is a name for a place, not for a physical behaviour. After a radiator swap and an insulation job it is, thermally, a different floor under the same name. A model fitted to version 1 and joined to version 3's readings is not stale data; it is the wrong asset. That is why practitioner session 1 keeps the asset record (the floor as built and maintained) apart from the model record (the model fitted to it), joined by a configuration version.

The check that replaces the habit. Every twin run names its asset and its configuration version, and every model names the version it was calibrated on. When the two differ, the result is flagged before anyone reads it. The AIAA and AIA (2020) "As Maintained" phase is this idea in their vocabulary: the twin follows the asset through its changes, not just its drawings.

Self-studySlowly changing dimensions, and why they are not enough3 min▶

Warehouse people already know the shape of this problem: a customer moves house, and a slowly changing dimension keeps both addresses with dates. The pattern carries over, and leader session 6 counts it among the things that do. What does not carry over is the trigger. A customer's move arrives as a write from a system; a retrofit arrives as nothing at all. The version table is only as good as the habit of filling it, and for a building that habit belongs to facilities, not to the data team.

So the version table needs a second line of defence: the surprises. On Kittiwake's bench, floor 4's mean surprise moved from +0.015 C in week 1 to +0.638 C in week 2 (a shift test score of 11.44, against under 0.7 on every other floor). That is the building filing the missing change request for you. Detectors for such shifts belong to learn anomaly detection.

Part 2 · covers the ground truth and the feedback loop

No label to score against, and data the twin helped create

13 min live

The next two mechanisms are about how you know you are right. A supervised model is scored against labels that sit still. A twin is scored against a room nobody can read exactly, and the data it is scored on next week exists partly because of what it advised this week. LiveMechanism 3 · there is no ground truth to label against5 min▶

The warehouse habit. Hold out some labelled rows and score the model against them. The label is the answer, it is stored, and the score is an accuracy.

What breaks. The true room temperature is never stored anywhere. The sensor is one noisy reading at one spot; on Kittiwake's constructed bench it is 0.404 C off the true room on average. The filtered twin, which combines the sensor with the physics of the floor, is 0.218 C off: closer than the instrument you would have used as the label. At its default setting the widget shows every floor's sensor between 0.373 and 0.433 C off and every floor's twin between 0.209 and 0.236 C off. If you had scored the twin against the sensor, you would have marked its better answer as error.

The anti-lever. Tell the twin its sensors are perfect and it copies them: 0.403 C off, no better than the sensors, and its 95 percent band now holds reality only 77.4 percent of the time, against 96.8 percent for the honest twin. Nothing in a sensor-scored dashboard would show the difference, because the overconfident twin agrees with the sensors beautifully.

The check that replaces the habit. Score what you can observe, honestly: the surprises (are they small, centred and patternless?) and the band's coverage on readings it did not see. Treat the sensor as evidence with its own error, never as the label. Sensor triage itself (drift, clocks, gaps) belongs to learn IoT data.

LiveMechanism 4 · the twin's advice changes the building4 min▶

The warehouse habit. The data is history. A churn model reads last year's customers, and nothing it says changes what last year was.

What breaks. In Kittiwake's story, the setback week happens because the twin advised it: 15 C at night and at weekends. Every reading from week 2 was produced by a building running the twin's advice. The next calibration, the next surprise and the next score are all computed on data the twin helped create. The National Academies (2024) describe the two-way interaction between virtual and physical as "central to the digital twin", and conclude it forms a feedback loop of data-driven model updating.

The sharpest consequence: the week without the setback never happens in a real building. Kittiwake's bench can report a true saving of 20.1 percent (6,343 to 5,067 kWh, constructed) only because the engine runs the same invented week twice. In a real building that counterfactual is gone the moment the advice is taken, so the twin's own estimate (21.5 percent calibrated, 23.9 percent from the drawings) cannot be checked against a measured "without".

The check that replaces the habit. Log each decision next to the data it produced, so later analysis knows which readings were made under which advice, and agree before acting how the effect will be judged. Practitioner session 9 closes the loop in full.

Self-studyWhy the filter beats the sensor, in one paragraph3 min▶

The filtered twin uses the method set out by Kalman (1960): predict the room from the physics, then correct the prediction toward each reading by a weight that depends on how much each is trusted. Because the physics knows rooms do not jump 0.4 C in an hour, it discards much of the sensor's noise; because the reading arrives every hour, the physics cannot wander far. Neither source alone is the truth; the blend is closer to it than either. Practitioner session 4 does it by hand. The National Academies (2024) also ask for verification, validation and uncertainty quantification to be continual and to adapt as the physical counterpart changes, which is the formal name for not trusting last month's score.

Part 3 · build-along

The cost-of-being-wrong map

★ 17 min · everyone builds

You will leave with one page your team can pin above a twin project: five rows, one per mechanism, each naming the warehouse habit, what breaks, and the check that replaces it. The fifth row is the one that changes how carefully you run the other four. Draw it on paper as you go; the figure is the finished map for Kittiwake.

Row 1 · write down who changes the asset

Name the people who can physically change the thing the twin mirrors (facilities, contractors, operators), and where, if anywhere, they record it. If the answer is "nowhere the data team can read", that is the first gap on your map. On Kittiwake's bench, one unrecorded job made floor 4's plan 63.1 percent wrong.

Row 2 · decide what identifies the asset

Write the identity as a pair: the asset's name plus its configuration version. Then check one thing: does the model record say which version it was calibrated on? If the model has no version, it cannot know it is out of date.

Row 3 · replace "accuracy" with two observable checks

Cross out any line on your plan that scores the twin against the sensors as if they were the truth. Write in its place: surprises watched per asset over time, and band coverage on readings the twin had not seen. Kittiwake's honest twin covered 96.8 percent; the twin told its sensors were perfect covered 77.4 percent and looked just as good on a sensor-scored dashboard.

Row 4 · log decisions as data

For each decision the twin informs, write where it will be recorded, with its date and the asset it touched, so later readings can be read as "data under this advice". Agree now how the decision's effect will be judged, because the week without it will never be measured.

Row 5 · price the cost of being wrong, then set the strictness

For your twin, write what a wrong answer costs and who bears it: comfort (a cold seminar room), energy (a heating plan that misses by a margin), safety (anything where people or equipment are at risk). Then set rows 1 to 4 to match: the higher the cost, the more often the surprises are reviewed and the fewer people may act on the twin unaided. Who may act is practitioner session 8. Safety, building codes and compliance are outside tonight's scope; take those to the people accountable for them.

The habit to keep. When a twin project inherits a warehouse plan, read it row by row and ask of each line: does this assume a system of record, a stable key, a stored label, data that sits still, or a cheap mistake? Every "yes" is a line to rewrite with the check from the map.

Check yourself

Three questions

Why is there no ground truth to score a building twin against?

The thing you would label against is unobservable. Scoring the twin against the sensor would mark its better answer as error; score the surprises and the band's coverage instead.

Kittiwake's twin advised a night setback, and the building ran it in week 2. Why does that feedback loop matter to anyone judging the twin?

The next data depends on the last decision. Only the constructed bench can run the same week twice; a real building loses the counterfactual when the advice is taken, so decisions must be logged next to the data they cause.

Why is "floor 4" not enough to identify the asset a twin describes?

A row key means the same thing forever; an asset's name does not. On Kittiwake's bench, an unlogged insulation job left the model describing the old floor 4, and its plan went 63.1 percent wrong.

Before session 6

Homework

Twenty minutes, on one plan you already own.

Run the map on one real plan

Take any data or AI project plan you have touched (a warehouse, a forecast, a model) and imagine its subject were a physical asset. Mark each line that assumes a system of record, a stable key, a stored label, data that sits still or a cheap mistake. Count them; leader session 6 sorts which can be kept.

Price one wrong answer

For one asset you know, write one sentence each on what a wrong twin answer would cost in comfort, energy and safety, and who would notice first. Keep it to what you know; it is a planning note, not a safety assessment.
