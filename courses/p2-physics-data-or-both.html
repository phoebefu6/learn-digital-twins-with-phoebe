Physics, data or both

Every twin has a model inside it, and that model was built one of three ways: from physics alone, from data alone, or from physics with a few numbers fitted to data. On a normal week all three can look fine. Tonight you watch them part on Kittiwake Hall, a constructed six-storey teaching building, in the week its facilities team switches on a night setback the records have never seen. Then you fit a grey-box parameter yourself in twenty lines of Python.

Part 0

The model inside Kittiwake's twin

Last session gave Kittiwake Hall (constructed) a model record: structure, version, parameters, provenance. Tonight is about the first field of that record. Is the structure physics someone wrote down, a pattern a fitting routine found, or physics with a few values learned from the meters? The answer decides what the twin can be trusted to say about a schedule nobody has run yet, which is the only question the facilities team really cares about.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A clear picture of white, black and grey box models and what each one needs: drawings, data, or both. A live comparison on Kittiwake floor 1 in which a regression that looks good in the week it learned from falls apart in the setback week, while the grey box holds. LiveThree ways to build the model3 min▶

A white box model is written from first principles: walls, windows and air volumes go in, equations come out, and every number comes from the drawings. A black box model knows no physics at all: it learns a mapping from inputs to the next reading from whatever data it is given. A grey box keeps the physical structure and lets the data set a few of its values. The difference that matters in a twin is not accuracy on last week. It is what each one does when tomorrow looks unlike anything in its record.

Real world

The AIAA and AIA (2020) position paper on digital twins says the virtual representation can be driven purely by data, purely by physics, or by a hybrid of the two. It does not rank them. That choice is left to whoever builds the twin, which is why it belongs in the model record with a reason next to it.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • RC network. A building drawn as an electrical circuit: thermal resistances (R) for heat paths, capacitances (C) for things that store heat. Kittiwake's twin uses two nodes per floor, room air and building mass.
  • Open loop. Run the model forward on its own predictions, with no sensor correction. Tonight every model runs open loop, so you see the model and not the filter.
  • One-step-ahead. Predict the next hour from the real current reading. Easy to score well on; it hides how errors compound.
  • Interpolation. Predicting inside the range of conditions the model was fitted on.
  • Extrapolation. Predicting outside it: a new schedule, a colder week, a retrofit.
  • Identifiable. A parameter the data can pin down. Practitioner session 3 shows a week of data pins down heat loss but not thermal mass.
Part 1 · covers white, black and grey box models

White box, black box, grey box

10 min live

Here are the three models of the same floor, Kittiwake floor 1, drawn side by side. The white box and the grey box have the same circuit; they differ only in where two numbers came from. The black box has no circuit at all, just six weights learned from a week of hourly rows.

LiveWhat each one needs4 min▶

White box: the drawings. No data at all, which is why it is the only option before a building is occupied. The price is the as-built gap: Kittiwake's real floors lose heat 1.15 to 1.45 times faster than the drawings say, and the design-spec twin is off by 2.35 to 5.81 C per floor in the setback week (constructed). Leader session 3 is about that gap.

Black box: data, and the right data. No drawings, no physics, no engineer. It needs hourly records that already contain the conditions you will ask it about, because it has no other way to know what they do.

Grey box: both. The drawings fix the shape (two nodes, three heat paths); the data sets the few values the drawings got wrong. Fewer numbers to fit than a black box, and every one of them means something you can check on site.

LiveA grey box in the literature3 min▶
Real world

Bacher and Madsen (2011), in Energy and Buildings, set out a procedure for choosing a grey-box model of a building's heat dynamics. As reported in summaries of the paper, the models are stochastic differential equations with RC structure, and the procedure starts small and adds nodes one at a time, keeping a bigger model only when a likelihood-ratio test says the data supports it. Kittiwake's two-node twin is a small member of that family.

The point to carry: grey box is not "a bit of physics sprinkled on machine learning". It is a physical model whose parameters are estimated statistically, with a rule for when the data justifies more structure.

Self-studyWhere neural surrogates sit2 min▶

A neural network trained to imitate a simulator, or to learn a building straight from its meters, is a black box with more capacity. More capacity helps it fit what it has seen; it does not tell it anything about what it has not. Physics-informed variants put equations back in the loss and move toward grey. They are named here only; the same question decides whether to trust one: was the regime you are asking about in its training data, or in its structure?

Part 2 · covers interpolation, extrapolation and the setback week

Extrapolation is where they part

7 min live

In week 1 Kittiwake runs its normal schedule: 21 C on weekday days, 19 C otherwise. In week 2 the real building turns on a night setback to 15 C. Fit the black box on week 1 only, give the grey box its week-1 calibration, and run all three forward through both weeks with no sensor correction. The widget does it in your browser.

Black box: least squares on week 1 only (hours 24 to 168), predicting the next reading from a constant, the current reading, outdoor temperature, heat meter, sun and timetable, then run on its own predictions through both weeks. LiveReading floor 14 min▶

Week 1, the week everyone tests on. The white box is 4.314 C off the true room: the drawings are wrong about this floor. The black box is 0.414 C off and the grey box 0.257 C. If you stopped here, the regression looks nearly as good as the physics, with no engineer needed.

Week 2, the week that matters. The grey box goes to 0.545 C. The black box goes to 2.133 C, five times its week-1 error. The white box, still carrying the drawings' error, goes to 5.657 C. Pick another floor: the black box's week-2 error sits between 1.908 and 2.245 C on every floor, the grey box's between 0.295 and 0.620 C. Your numbers will differ on another world; the shape is the lesson.

Not every floor is a clean win. On floor 2 the drawings happen to be closer, and the black box (2.080 C) edges the white box (2.352 C) in week 2. The grey box still beats both. The data can rescue the physics, and the physics can rescue the data; neither alone is safe.

LiveWhy the black box breaks: two mechanisms3 min▶

It never saw the regime. In week 1 floor 1's room never went below 16.5 C. In the setback week it reaches 12.9 C. A regression is a summary of the rows it was fitted on; outside them it is a guess with the confidence of a formula.

It cannot see the hidden state. The widget prints the fitted weights: on floor 1 the regression keeps about half of the current reading (weight 0.525) and adds a constant of about 7.08 C. That constant is quietly standing in for the building mass, which sat in one band all of week 1. In week 2 the mass cools by a couple of degrees, and a constant cannot follow it. The grey box carries the mass as a state, so it can.

Both mechanisms have the same fix: either put the regime into the training data, or put the physics into the model.

Self-studyThe trap of scoring one step ahead3 min▶

Score the same regression one hour ahead, feeding it the real reading each hour, and it looks much better, because every hour starts from the truth plus sensor noise. A twin asked "what will this schedule use next week?" never gets those readings: it runs on its own predictions for a week. Score the model the way it will be used. The widget runs every model open loop for exactly that reason.

Real world

The National Academies (2024) report concludes that verification, validation and uncertainty quantification for twins must be continual and adapt to changes in the physical counterpart. A schedule change is such a change. A model validated on the old schedule is validated on the old schedule, and the check has to be run again.

Part 3 · covers choosing a model for the job

Choosing

3 min live

Two questions settle most of it. What do you have: drawings, data, or both? And will the twin be asked about conditions outside its record: a new schedule, a retrofit, a colder winter? The grid answers both at once.

LiveHybrid models in practice1 min▶

Most working building twins sit in the copper cell, and "hybrid" covers more than grey box. Two common shapes: a physical model with fitted parameters (tonight's grey box), and a physical model with a data-driven correction trained on its residuals. The second is only as safe as the residual model is on the new regime, so the same extrapolation question applies to the correction.

Real world

The National Academies (2024) report concludes a twin should be defined at a fidelity and resolution that make it fit for purpose. Read that as a model-choice rule: the question the twin must answer decides how much physics it needs, not the other way round.

LiveWhen a black box is fine1 min▶

When the twin is only asked about conditions well inside its record, and the record is long. A model that forecasts tomorrow's load under the same schedule, trained on several winters, is interpolating, and a black box may well beat a hand-tuned physical model there. Forecasting methods for that job, and how to test them honestly, live in learn time-series forecasting.

Self-studyPhysics gives you parameters you can audit2 min▶

A grey box's fitted kU of 1.50 on floor 1 says something about the building: this floor loses heat about one and a half times faster than drawn (constructed; true 1.42). A facilities engineer can go and look for the reason. The black box's weight of 0.525 on the current reading says nothing anyone can check on site. Leader session 3 builds on this: the gap between the drawn and the built building is itself a finding, and only a physical model gives it a name.

The grey box's kC of 1.62 against a true 0.85 is a warning, not a finding. A week of data could not pin the mass down. Practitioner session 3 shows why, and why the twin still predicted the setback week well.

Build-along

Fit a grey box in Python

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You will build a grey box from scratch: the physics is fixed, one number is unknown, and the data finds it. To see whether the fit works you need to know the answer, so you generate a toy two-day record from a known heat-loss multiplier, add sensor noise, and check the search gets it back. The figure is the loop you will write.

Write the one-hour step function

This is practitioner session 1's step function with one change: the two heat paths to outdoors are multiplied by kU, the heat-loss multiplier you will fit. Everything else stays from the drawings.

fit.py, part 1# Grey-box fit: the physics is fixed, one parameter (kU) is found from data. import random def step(ti, tm, to, q, kU, Ci=6.0, Cm=20.0, Rim=0.5, Rio=10.0, Rmo=4.0): for _ in range(4): dti = ((tm - ti) / Rim + (to - ti) * kU / Rio + q) / Ci dtm = ((ti - tm) / Rim + (to - tm) * kU / Rmo) / Cm ti, tm = ti + 0.25 * dti, tm + 0.25 * dtm return ti, tm

Generate a toy two-day record with a known answer

Fixed inputs you choose: a still 5 C outside, 8 kW of heat from 07:00 to 19:00. The "real" floor has kU 1.3. Add sensor noise from a seeded generator so everyone gets the same record.

fit.py, part 2def simulate(kU, hours=48): ti, tm, out = 20.0, 19.0, [20.0] for h in range(hours): to = 5.0 # a still, cold two days q = 8.0 if 7 <= h % 24 < 19 else 0.0 # heat on 07:00 to 19:00 ti, tm = step(ti, tm, to, q, kU) out.append(ti) return out # A toy record with a known answer: kU = 1.3, plus sensor noise. rng = random.Random(1) record = [t + rng.gauss(0.0, 0.4) for t in simulate(1.3)]

Grid-search kU from 0.8 to 2.0

Twenty-five guesses, 0.05 apart. For each, simulate the two days and measure how far the simulation is from the record. Print every fourth guess so you can see the error fall and rise.

fit.py, part 3def rmse(a, b): return (sum((x - y) ** 2 for x, y in zip(a, b)) / len(a)) ** 0.5 best = None for i in range(25): # kU from 0.80 to 2.00 by 0.05 kU = round(0.8 + 0.05 * i, 2) r = rmse(simulate(kU), record) if best is None or r < best[1]: best = (kU, r) if i % 4 == 0: print(f"kU {kU:.2f} rmse {r:.3f} C")

Print the best kU and run it

Save all three parts as fit.py in a scratch folder and run python3 fit.py. This is the exact output of that file.

fit.py, part 4print(f"best kU {best[0]:.2f}, rmse {best[1]:.3f} C (true kU 1.30)")
OutputkU 0.80 rmse 1.986 C kU 1.00 rmse 1.146 C kU 1.20 rmse 0.456 C kU 1.40 rmse 0.539 C kU 1.60 rmse 1.138 C kU 1.80 rmse 1.733 C kU 2.00 rmse 2.291 C best kU 1.30, rmse 0.345 C (true kU 1.30)

Read it

The search found the true 1.30. The error is a valley: steep on both sides, lowest at the answer. Notice the best RMSE, 0.345 C, is about the size of the sensor noise you added; a fit cannot get much below the noise without fitting the noise itself. Now break it: change the seed, or set the noise to 1.0, or fit on 12 hours instead of 48, and watch how sure the valley stays. Run it and read the output; your numbers will differ. Practitioner session 3 adds the second parameter, kC, and finds a valley that is not a valley.

The habit to keep. Before you trust a fitting routine on real data, run it on toy data where you know the answer. If it cannot find a number you planted, it will not find the one in the building.

Check yourself

Three questions

Kittiwake switches on a 15 C night setback that never appeared in its week-1 record. Which model held up best on floor 1 (constructed)?

The black box was close in week 1 and five times worse in week 2. The white box extrapolates, but carries the drawings' wrong values with it (5.657 C). The grey box has both the structure and the fitted values.

What does a grey-box model fit from data?

The circuit comes from physics; the data sets values like kU and kC. That is why a fitted kU of 1.50 can be checked on site and a regression weight cannot.

Why does a black box need the regime you will ask about in its training data?

The regression summarises week 1. Its constant stood in for a mass that sat in one band; in the setback week the mass cooled and the constant could not follow.

Take this with you

Cheat sheet

White box. Physics only; every value from the drawings. Needs no data, inherits the as-built gap.
Black box. Data only; no structure. Fine inside its record, a guess outside it.
Grey box. Physical structure, a few parameters fitted from data. Needs both.
Score it as you use it. Open loop for planning; one-step-ahead flatters.
Extrapolation test. Hold out a week unlike the training week, never a random sample.
Hidden states. What no sensor reads (the mass) a regression folds into a constant.
Audit the parameters. A fitted kU means something on site; a regression weight does not.
Plant an answer. Test any fit on toy data with a known value first. Kittiwake is constructed; your numbers will differ.
