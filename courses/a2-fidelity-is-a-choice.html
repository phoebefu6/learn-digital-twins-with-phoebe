Fidelity is a choice, not a virtue

The most detailed twin is not the best one. The right twin is the coarsest model that still answers the decision, because every node, room and parameter you add is one more thing that has to be kept in step with the real building. Kittiwake Hall is a constructed teaching building, and every Kittiwake number on this page is generated by the engine in your browser, not observed. Tonight is how to size a twin from the decision it serves, what extra detail really costs, and a one-page fidelity spec you can write before anyone buys anything. Energy and savings figures here are constructed teaching numbers; nothing here is investment advice.

Kittiwake Hall, the week someone asks for more detail

Kittiwake's twin (constructed) is two nodes per floor: the room air and the building's mass, six floors, twelve fitted parameters. It plans next week's heating within 1.7 percent of what the building really used. Then a meeting happens. Someone has seen a render with every room coloured by temperature and asks why Kittiwake's twin cannot do that. Someone else wants airflow. The analyst from the warehouse knows the instinct: more columns, more rows, more truth. In a twin that instinct is expensive, because every extra detail has to be measured, fitted and kept in sync, forever. Tonight is how to answer that meeting.

LiveFidelity and resolution, in plain words3 min▶

Fidelity is how much of the real asset's behaviour the model reproduces. Resolution is how finely it divides the asset up: one building, one floor, one room, one cubic metre of air. They travel together, and both have a price: each new piece of the model needs its own parameters, its own data, and its own check that it still matches.

The professional norm is to set both from the decision. A twin that answers next week's heating plan per floor does not need to know which seminar room is cold, and pretending it does is worse than admitting it cannot.

Real world

The National Academies (2024) report on digital twins concludes (Conclusion 3-1) that a twin should be defined at a level of fidelity and resolution that makes it "fit for purpose". Not as detailed as possible, not as detailed as the vendor can render: fit for the purpose someone names.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Node. One place in the model that has its own temperature. Kittiwake's twin has two per floor: the room air and the mass of the floor.
  • Parameter. A number that says how the asset behaves: kU scales how fast a floor loses heat, kC scales how much heat its mass stores. Both are fitted from readings.
  • Grey box. A model whose structure comes from physics (heat flows from warm to cold through resistances) and whose parameters come from data. Practitioner session 2 builds one.
  • Identifiability. Whether the data you have can pin a parameter down. If two very different values fit the readings equally well, the parameter is not identified, however precise the number printed.
  • Fit for purpose. Detailed enough to answer the decision, and no more.
  • Constructed. Kittiwake Hall and every number about it are invented so that the truth is knowable. The research and standards are real and cited.
Part 1 · covers fit for purpose, and how the decision sets the resolution

Fit for purpose

15 min live

Draw the same building four ways, from coarse to fine. Each step down the ladder answers a sharper question, and each step down adds things that must be measured and kept in sync. The work is to find the rung where the decision is answered, and stop there.

LiveThe decision sets the resolution6 min▶

Start with the sentence from last session's homework: "we will use the twin to decide X, by Y, every Z". Kittiwake's is "we will decide each Friday whether to run a night setback on each floor next week". That sentence sets three things at once.

  • The unit. The decision is per floor, so the model needs floors, not rooms.
  • The horizon. A week ahead. What matters is how each floor gains and loses heat over days, not the airflow in the next ten minutes.
  • The accuracy that changes the answer. The plan flips only if the estimate is wrong by more than the difference between the options. In the constructed bench the two-node twin estimates the setback week's heat 1.7 percent high: nowhere near enough to flip the choice.

Each of those is something a leader can state without knowing any physics. Each rules out detail that would cost money to build and more money to keep true.

Real world

The National Academies (2024) tie fitness for purpose to the decision a twin informs, and ask for verification, validation and uncertainty quantification to continue for the life of the twin. Read together, the cost of a detail is not paid once at build time; it is paid at every check, for as long as the twin is trusted.

Self-studyWhy "more realistic" is the wrong question3 min▶

Realism is about how the model looks to people; fidelity is about which behaviours it reproduces. A twin that colours every room by temperature from one sensor per floor looks realistic and is inventing the room colours. A two-node floor model looks like nothing, and answers its decision within a couple of percent. When someone asks for more realism, ask which decision it would change, and what would have to be measured to keep it true.

Self-studyWhen the decision changes, the rung changes3 min▶

A twin sized for one decision is not automatically fit for the next. If Kittiwake's facilities lead later wants to answer complaints about cold rooms, the honest answer is not to subdivide the floor model on paper; it is to add room sensors first, then a room-level model, then a check that it still matches. Where to put those sensors is a placement problem of its own; city-scale and placement questions live in learn smart city, sensor and sampling questions in learn IoT data.

Part 2 · covers why every extra detail must be fitted, measured and kept true

Every detail is a promise to keep

10 min live

Each node in a grey-box model brings parameters, and each parameter has to be found from readings. Even Kittiwake's small twin shows the limit: one week of data fixes how fast a floor loses heat, but not how much heat its mass stores. Add nodes and that problem grows faster than the model does. Spot a floor that changed. In the break run, floor 4 is retrofitted at the start of week 2 and nobody tells the twin. Its surprises shift so far (z = 11.44) that floor 4 is flagged, and only floor 4; no other floor moves past 0.7. Rung 2 answers this one too, because the decision is per floor.

Say which room is cold. The twin has one sensor and one air temperature per floor. It cannot say which room is cold, and the widget prints no number, because any number would be invented. That is the honest answer: this decision needs rung 3, which needs room sensors Kittiwake does not have.

Self-studyWhy more nodes is not free4 min▶

Kittiwake's two-node twin fits two numbers per floor from one week of hourly readings. kU, how fast heat leaks out, comes back within 0.08 of the truth on every floor. kC, how much heat the mass stores, does not: floor 3's is fitted at 1.68 against a true 0.89. The week's readings simply do not contain enough slow change to tell a heavy floor from a light one. The plan is still good, because the plan mostly depends on heat loss.

Now imagine one node per room. Every room adds its own pair of numbers, plus the heat flowing between neighbouring rooms, while the data grows only if you buy a sensor for every room. Parameters the data cannot pin down get printed with two decimal places anyway. Practitioner session 3 does the fitting by hand and shows the flat valley where kC hides.

Real world

Bacher and Madsen (2011) describe identifying grey-box models of a building's heat dynamics by starting small and adding model complexity (more nodes) one step at a time, keeping a step only when a likelihood-ratio test says the data support it (method reported from secondary summaries). The discipline is the same as tonight's: detail has to earn its place against the data, not just the drawing.

Self-studyThe standards view3 min▶

ISO 23247-1:2021 sets out a digital twin framework for manufacturing (reported: the standard is paywalled; its contents list was read). It frames a twin around observable manufacturing elements: the things on the shop floor that can actually be observed. The idea carries over to buildings even though the scope does not: a twin should be defined around what you can observe, and detail you cannot observe is detail you cannot keep in sync.

AIAA and AIA (2020) name three essential elements: a virtual representation, a physical asset, and the transfer of data between them. Fidelity lives in the first; the second and third decide whether that fidelity can be kept honest. The paper also notes that models can be data-driven, physics-based or a hybrid of the two; Kittiwake's grey box is the hybrid.

Neither document gives a rule for how detailed a twin should be. The professional norm is the one above: the decision sets it.

Part 3 · build-along

Write a one-page fidelity spec

★ 17 min · everyone builds

You will leave with one page that sizes a twin before anyone builds or buys it: the decision, the horizon, the coarsest model that answers it, the sensors that model needs, and the signal that would justify more detail. The figure is Kittiwake's, filled in. Write yours for an asset you know.

Write the decision in one sentence

Who decides what, how often. "Decide each Friday whether to run a night setback on each floor next week." If you cannot write it, stop: no fidelity can be fit for a purpose nobody has named.

Write the horizon

How far ahead the decision looks and how often it is revisited. A week-ahead plan does not need minute-by-minute data; a safety interlock would. The horizon tells you how often the twin must be checked against the asset.

Name the coarsest model that answers it

Walk down the ladder from the top and stop at the first rung that answers the decision. For Kittiwake that is two nodes per floor. Write why the rung above is too coarse and why the rung below is not needed.

List the sensors that model needs

Every node needs data to stay true. List each feed, its interval, and who owns it. If a feed on the list does not exist yet, the rung you chose is not yet available; that is a budget line, not a modelling detail.

Write what would make you add detail

The trigger, stated in advance: a new decision that needs a finer unit, or evidence that the current rung no longer answers the old one. Write it down now, so that the next request for more detail is judged against it rather than against a render.

The habit to keep. When someone asks for a more detailed twin, ask which decision the extra detail would change, what would have to be measured to keep it true, and who will pay for that every month. If no decision changes, the coarser twin was the right one.

Check yourself

Three questions

The National Academies (2024) say a twin's fidelity and resolution should make it fit for purpose. What sets the purpose?

A twin informs decisions; the decision sets the unit, the horizon and the accuracy that matters. Detail beyond that is cost without value.

Kittiwake's constructed twin fits kU within 0.08 on every floor but fits floor 3's kC at 1.68 against a true 0.89. What does that say about adding more nodes?

One week of readings fixes heat loss but not the mass. More nodes multiply parameters faster than the data grows, unless you also buy more sensors and keep them running.

Which of these decisions needs room-level data that Kittiwake's per-floor twin does not have?

The per-floor twin plans within 1.7 percent and flagged floor 4 alone after the unlogged retrofit. It has one sensor per floor, so any statement about a single room would be invented.
