Reading a digital twin claim

Every twin arrives with a sentence attached: it is live, it is real-time, it is accurate, it saved this much. Each of those sentences can be true and still mislead. Kittiwake Hall, a constructed six-storey teaching building whose every number is generated in the page rather than observed, gives us a twin that claims its night setback cut heating by 24 percent. The true saving in the constructed world is 20.1 percent. Tonight is how to read a claim like that, what to ask for, and why the honest answer always comes with a baseline. Savings are money, so one line for the whole page: nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Kittiwake Hall, the slide that says 24 percent

Kittiwake Hall is constructed: six floors of seminar rooms, a heat meter and a room sensor per floor, a weather feed and a set of drawings. Three sessions ago the facilities lead asked for a twin to plan a night setback; last session showed how far the drawings sit from the building as built. Now the setback has run for a week and a slide lands on the lead's desk: "Our twin cut heating 24 percent." It has a striking 3D picture of the building, the word "real-time" twice and a tracking error of under half a degree. The lead has to decide whether to repeat the setback across the estate and report the saving upward. The slide is built from Kittiwake's design twin, and every sentence on it can be checked.

LiveWhy a claim needs reading at all3 min▶

A claim about a twin compresses three different things into one sentence: what the model is, how well it agrees with the asset, and what someone decided because of it. A render, a tracking number and a savings figure each answer one of those, and none of them answers the others. Reading a claim means pulling the sentence apart and asking for the evidence behind each piece.

Real world

The National Academies (2024) report on digital twins, read at source, names verification, validation and uncertainty quantification as an area of particular need, and concludes that for a twin they have to be a continual process that adapts as the physical counterpart changes. A claim that was true at handover is a claim about a building that may no longer exist. The leader's job is to ask when it was last checked, and against what.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Render. A picture of the asset, often 3D. It can be beautiful and still have no data link and no check.
  • Real-time. A word about how often data arrives. It says nothing about whether the model still agrees with the asset.
  • Tracking error. How far the twin's estimate of a measured quantity (here, room temperature) sits from the truth or from the readings. Small tracking error is necessary, not sufficient.
  • Band and coverage. A band is the range the twin says reality will fall in; coverage is how often it actually did. A 95 percent band should hold the reading about 95 percent of the time.
  • Counterfactual. What would have happened without the change: the heat Kittiwake would have used with no setback. In a real building nobody measures it; it is modelled.
  • Baseline. The number a saving is measured against: the counterfactual, as some model estimated it.
  • CV(RMSE) and NMBE. Two scores for how well a model matches metered energy: the spread of the errors, and their average bias, both as a percentage of the mean.
Part 1 · covers render versus live model, and what "connected" means

A render is not a twin

12 min live

The fastest way to make a model look like a twin is to make it look like the building. A render shows geometry, finishes and sometimes colours painted by a value, and it can be updated every second from a feed. None of that tells you whether the model behind it agrees with the asset. The figure puts the same Kittiwake floor side by side, shown two ways; the difference is not in the picture.

LiveWhat a render can show, and what it cannot5 min▶

A render can show geometry, where things are, which room a reading belongs to, and a value painted onto a surface. That is useful for orientation and for talking to people who do not read charts.

A render cannot show whether the value painted on the wall came from a model that still agrees with the wall. It cannot show the model's parameters, when they were last fitted, or how wide its uncertainty is. A render of Kittiwake's design twin would look identical to a render of its calibrated twin, and the design twin is 4.45 C off the true room.

Real world

The National Academies (2024) put it as a finding: a digital twin is "more than just simulation and modeling." What it adds is the two-way link with the physical counterpart and the dynamic updating from its data. Neither is visible in a picture, which is why a picture is the cheapest way to claim both.

LiveWhat "connected" should mean4 min▶

"Connected" is a claim with two parts, and a leader should hear both as numbers. An interval: how often data from the asset reaches the model, from which feeds, and whether it updates the state, the parameters or both. Kittiwake's twin is updated hourly from one room sensor and one heat meter per floor. A check: how often the model's predictions are compared with what the asset did, and who looks at the result. Kittiwake's check is the hourly surprise, the reading minus the prediction, per floor.

A feed with no check is plumbing. A check nobody reads is a log file. The promise from leader session 1 needs both, and an owner for the second.

Self-studyWhy the physical asset is in the definition3 min▶

The AIAA and AIA (2020) position paper, read at source, lists three essential elements: a virtual representation, a physical realization and a transfer of data between them, and draws the conclusion that a twin requires a physical asset. A render of a building that is still on paper can be a fine design model; by that definition it cannot yet be a twin, because there is nothing for it to stay in sync with. When a claim describes a twin "before construction", read it as a design model with a plan to become a twin, and ask when the data link starts.

Part 2 · covers real-time and accuracy claims, and savings figures

A small error and a big saving, read honestly

13 min live

Accuracy claims usually arrive as one number: the twin tracks the room within so many tenths of a degree. That number answers one question about one quantity on one stretch of data. The figure shows two ways it can be true and still mislead on Kittiwake's constructed floors: once about the twin's uncertainty, once about the plan. Then the widget turns to the number leaders are asked to repeat upward, the saving.

Live"Real-time" and "accurate": ask against what, on which data5 min▶

"Real-time" is a claim about the feed. Translate it into an interval and a check, as in Part 1. Kittiwake plans heating a week ahead, so an hourly update is plenty; a second-by-second feed would not make its plan better. Sensor clocks, sampling and gaps are their own subject, in learn IoT data.

"Accurate" needs three more words. Against what: room temperature, or the energy the plan depends on? On which data: the week the twin was calibrated on, or a different week? Including the band: how often did reality fall inside the range the twin gave? In the figure, the twin that trusts its sensors fully has a tracking error nearly the same as the sensors' own 0.404 C, which sounds fine, while its band fails one reading in four or five. Floor 4 after the retrofit tracks its room well because the filter follows the sensor, while its heat estimate is 63.1 percent wrong.

Real world

ASHRAE Guideline 14 (editions 2002 and 2014; paywalled, so reported here via secondary papers) is commonly cited for when a building energy model counts as calibrated: hourly CV(RMSE) at most 30 percent and NMBE within plus or minus 10 percent, or monthly 15 and 5 percent. Three things to notice. The criteria judge energy, not room temperature, so a temperature tracking claim does not meet them. Editions differ, and so do denominators and sign conventions, so ask which edition and which formula. And Garrett and New (2016) question whether these metrics are sufficient for calibration at all (reported). Meeting a threshold is a floor, not a verdict.

LiveReading the savings widget3 min▶

A saving is a comparison between two weeks of heat: the week with the change, which the meter measures, and the week without it, which nobody can measure because it did not happen. That second number is the counterfactual. In the constructed world the engine can run it, so the true saving is known: 6,343 kWh without the setback, 5,067 kWh with it, 20.1 percent.

A twin has to model the counterfactual. The design twin believes the building is cheaper to heat than it is: its own baseline is 4,471 kWh and its setback week 3,402 kWh, so it claims 23.9 percent. The calibrated twin's baseline is 6,565 kWh and it claims 21.5 percent: much closer, still a model output. Across the four constructed worlds the design twin overstates the true saving by 3.2 to 4.0 points and the calibrated twin by 0.6 to 1.5. The shape holds; the sizes differ. That spread is the reason a saving should travel with a band, not a single figure.

Self-studyWhy savings attributed to twins are contested4 min▶

Published savings attributed to digital twins vary widely, and they are argued over. The disagreement is not mainly about whether twins help. It is about method: what baseline was used and who modelled it, whether the change would have been made anyway without the twin, whether the saving was measured over a season or extrapolated from a week, and whether the people reporting it also sold or built the twin. Any one of these can move a figure by more than the effect being claimed.

The honest position is that a savings figure from the literature tells you little until you know its baseline and its band, and that no single percentage summarises what twins save. Kittiwake's constructed figures show the mechanism with the truth known; in a real building the truth is not known, and the counterfactual is always a model. Not legal or compliance advice; measurement and verification protocols exist for exactly this, and your organisation's reporting rules apply.

Part 3 · build-along

A checklist for any twin claim

★ 17 min · everyone builds

You will leave with a six-question checklist that fits on one card and works on any twin claim, from any sector, whether it comes from your own team or from outside. Then you will run it on the slide from Part 0, "Our twin cut heating 24 percent", and see why the honest answer is 20.1 percent with a baseline. The first figure is the saving drawn as it really is: two weeks of heat, one of them imagined.

Ask which asset, and which version

Write the asset and the date of the last physical change the twin knows about. The slide says "our twin". Pressed, it is Kittiwake's design twin, built from the drawings and never fitted to readings: the version from before the building existed.

Ask whether it is a render or a live model

Cover the picture with your hand and ask what is left: a data link, a check, a band? If nothing is left, it was a render. Kittiwake's design twin receives readings but its parameters were never moved by them, so its render and its numbers both describe the drawings.

Ask for the update interval and the check

Replace "real-time" with an interval and a named check. For Kittiwake the honest answer is hourly, with an hourly surprise per floor. Then ask who reads the surprises and when they last did.

Ask what the accuracy is against, and on which data

Temperature or energy? The calibration week or a different week? With a band, and how often did it hold? Write the slide's answer next to Part 2's traps: a twin can be 0.403 C off with a band that held only 77.4 percent, or track floor 4 within 0.399 C while its heat estimate is 63.1 percent wrong. If the accuracy is not about energy, it says nothing about a saving.

Ask for the saving's baseline and band

A saving is one minus the measured week over the modelled week. Ask for both numbers in kWh, who modelled the baseline, and how wide its band is. For the slide, the design twin's own baseline is 4,471 kWh, well below what the building would really have used, and that is where its 23.9 percent comes from. The constructed truth is 20.1 percent against a baseline of 6,343 kWh. The calibrated twin's 21.5 percent is the best a real team could report, and it should travel with a band and the word "modelled".

Ask who measured it

The meter readings for the setback week are measurements; the baseline is a model run; the percentage is arithmetic on both. Ask which parts were measured, by whom, and whether anyone who did not build the twin checked the arithmetic and the baseline. Then rewrite the slide: "Night setback, setback week: 5,067 kWh measured against a modelled 6,565 kWh baseline from the calibrated twin, a modelled saving of about 21 percent; band to follow." In the constructed world we can add the truth, 20.1 percent. In yours, nobody can, which is why the band matters.

The habit to keep. Never repeat a twin's saving upward without its baseline in kWh, the word "modelled" next to the baseline, and a band. If the claim cannot supply them, report the measured week and say the saving is not yet known.

Check yourself

Three questions

A team presents a photoreal, live-updating 3D view of a building as its digital twin. What is the first thing a leader should ask for?

A render shows the picture, not the model's agreement with the asset. The National Academies (2024) say a twin is more than simulation and modelling: it is updated from its physical counterpart and checked continually.

Kittiwake's twin told its sensors are perfect (constructed) has a narrower band than the honest twin. Why is that not a better twin?

A band is a promise about how often reality falls inside it. The over-confident twin copies the sensor noise (0.403 C off) and breaks that promise one reading in four or five.

Why does a twin's savings figure need a baseline?

The counterfactual is modelled. Kittiwake's design twin modelled it at 4,471 kWh and claimed 23.9 percent; against the constructed truth of 6,343 kWh the saving is 20.1 percent.

Before session 5

Homework

Twenty minutes, on one claim and one sentence of your own.

Run the six questions on one public claim

Pick any public description of a digital twin that states a result, from any sector. Write one line per question with what the claim says, and mark the questions it leaves unanswered. Do not judge the result; judge what you would need to believe it.

Rewrite one saving honestly

Take a savings figure you have reported or been shown, from a twin or any model. Rewrite it in the form "measured X against a modelled baseline Y from model Z, band W". Leave blanks where you do not know. The blanks are your questions for the next review.
