The twin sync bench

Everything from the practitioner track on one bench. Kittiwake Hall, a constructed six-storey teaching building, gets a thermal twin of every floor that runs in your browser: first straight from the drawings, then calibrated on a week of readings, then kept in sync by a Kalman filter. You ask it a planning question (what does a night setback save next week?) and read the answer the way sessions 3, 4 and 7 taught: an error against the true room, an energy estimate against what the building really used, and a band that says how far to trust both. Then you tell the filter the sensors are perfect and watch the band lie, and you insulate floor 4 without writing it down and watch the twin point at the change log. Everything here is synthetic, declared on the widget, and computed in the page.

Part 0

The question Kittiwake has to answer

Kittiwake Hall (constructed) sets its thermostats to 21 C on weekday days and 19 C the rest of the time; simple proportional thermostats, which settle below their setpoint (occupied hours average 18.6 C across the six floors in the setback week). The facilities lead wants to drop nights and weekends to 15 C and asks the twin one question: how much heat would next week use, and how sure are you? The building then runs the setback for real, so in this constructed world every answer can be checked against what happened, and against a true room temperature no sensor can see.

★ What you walk out with tonight. The whole ladder, computed in front of you on 14 constructed days of six floors. A twin built from the drawings is 4.45 C off the true room and estimates the setback week's heat 32.9 percent low, because the real floors lose heat faster than designed. Calibrated on one week of readings it is 0.48 C off and 1.7 percent high. Kept in sync by a Kalman filter it is 0.218 C off, better than the sensors' own 0.404 C, and its 24-hour what-if band covers 96.8 percent of what the sensors then read. An anti-lever that trusts the sensors fully: the twin copies their noise (0.403 C), the band narrows from 2.20 to 1.28 C and covers only 77.4 percent. A break button that insulates floor 4 without logging it: floor 4's energy estimate goes 63.1 percent wrong and its innovations jump from +0.015 to +0.638 C, which flags floor 4 and nothing else.

LiveWhat is real and what is constructed3 min▶

Real: the mathematics. A two-node resistance-capacitance model of a room and its building mass (the grey-box family Bacher and Madsen 2011 build up node by node), a grid-search calibration, the Kalman (1960) predict and update, covariance propagated into a 95 percent band, and an innovation test for a shift. The idea that a twin is "dynamically updated with data from its physical twin" is the National Academies (2024) definition, adapted from AIAA and AIA (2020).

Constructed, and declared on the widget: Kittiwake Hall, its six floors, the weather, the sun, the occupants, the as-built gap (real floors lose heat 1.15 to 1.45 times the drawings and hold 0.70 to 0.95 of the designed mass), the thermostats, the 0.4 C sensor noise, every reading. Nothing here describes a real building or product. In a constructed world the true room temperature is known, so you can see what the sensors and the twin get wrong.

LiveThe five runs worth doing first6 min▶
  • Leave everything alone. Tracking error against the true room in week 2: 4.450 C from the drawings, 0.482 C calibrated, 0.218 C with the filter. The sensors on their own are 0.404 C off: a good filter beats its own sensors because it also knows the physics.
  • Read the energy column. The building really used 5,067 kWh of heat in the setback week. The drawings say 3,402 (32.9 percent low); calibrated, 5,152 (1.7 percent high). The filter barely changes this number, because a week-ahead plan depends on the parameters, not on this morning's state.
  • Read the saving. Normal heating would have used 6,343 kWh, so the setback truly saved 20.1 percent. The design-spec twin says 23.9 percent; the calibrated twin 21.5 percent. A twin built from drawings oversells the saving.
  • Read the band. At the honest setting, each midnight of week 2 the twin forecasts the next 24 hours with a 95 percent band 2.20 C wide on average. It covers 96.8 percent of the readings that then arrive.
  • Try seeds 1, 7 and 42. The filter's error stays at 0.22 to 0.23 C, coverage at 97.4 to 97.9 percent, and the design twin is 31 to 36 percent low on energy every time. Quote seed-1124 numbers and say "your numbers move with the seed". What holds is the shape.
Self-studyWhy the filter beats its own sensors4 min▶

A reading is the room plus noise. A model prediction is the room plus the model's error. The Kalman filter blends the two in proportion to how much it trusts each: at the honest setting its gain on the room reading settles at about 0.445, so each hour the twin moves 44.5 percent of the way from its prediction toward the reading. The noise in one reading is mostly averaged away, while a real change that persists for hours gets through. That is why the twin's 0.218 C beats the sensors' 0.404 C here. Practitioner session 4 does the same arithmetic by hand.

Part 2 · covers the scorecard and what each rung fixes

Each rung fixes a different column

10 min live

A single accuracy number hides what a twin is for. The scorecard keeps three apart: does the twin know the state of the building now (tracking error), does it know how the building behaves (energy estimate), and does it know how much it does not know (band coverage). Calibration fixes the second, assimilation the first, and only an honest noise model gives you the third.

LiveReading the four columns5 min▶

The drawings are wrong in one direction. Every constructed floor loses heat faster than designed, so the design-spec twin thinks rooms are warmer than they are (4.45 C off) and that heating them costs less (32.9 percent low). Per floor it is 21.6 to 42.5 percent low. Leader session 3 is about why the building you drew is not the one you have.

Calibration fixes behaviour. Fitting two multipliers to a week of readings recovers each floor's heat-loss factor within 0.08 and brings the plan to 1.7 percent. It does not pin down the building's mass: one week of readings cannot tell a heavy floor from a light one (practitioner session 3).

Assimilation fixes state. The filter halves the tracking error, from 0.482 to 0.218 C, and leaves the week-ahead plan where calibration put it. If someone says "we added live data, so the forecast is better", ask which forecast.

The band is its own column. A twin can have a good tracking number and a dishonest band. The last row proves it.

Self-studyCalibration criteria you will hear quoted4 min▶

Building energy modellers often quote ASHRAE Guideline 14: a model is commonly treated as calibrated when hourly CV(RMSE) is at most 30 percent and NMBE within plus or minus 10 percent, monthly 15 and 5 percent. That is reported here from secondary papers, because the guideline is paywalled, and editions differ. Those criteria judge energy, not the room temperature this bench scores, and researchers have questioned whether they are enough on their own. Treat them as a floor for a conversation, not as proof the twin is fit for your purpose.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever and the break button

A lever that makes it worse, and a button that breaks it

10 min live

A bench that can only report good news is not a bench. The anti-lever tells the filter its sensors are almost perfect (0.01 C instead of the true 0.4 C), so its gain goes to 0.999 and the twin copies every reading. The break button insulates floor 4 at the start of week 2 and records it nowhere: the real floor now loses heat at 0.6 times its old rate, and the twin does not know.

LiveWhat the anti-lever teaches5 min▶

Press "Trust the sensors fully". The twin's tracking error rises from 0.218 to 0.403 C, which is the sensors' own error: with a gain of 0.999 the twin is a copy of the last reading, noise included, and the physics adds nothing. The what-if band narrows from 2.20 to 1.28 C, because the filter believes it knew the starting state exactly and that the thermostat reads a perfect sensor. Coverage falls from 96.8 to 77.4 percent: roughly one reading in four lands outside a band that claims 95.

Nothing else in the scorecard moves: the energy estimate is still 1.7 percent high. That is the trap. A narrower band looks like a more confident, better twin, and a dashboard would show it as one. The National Academies (2024) report calls uncertainty quantification an area of particular need for exactly this reason: a band is a claim, and claims need checking against what then happens.

Step down through 0.2 and 0.1 C to see it is a slope, not a cliff: coverage 88.5 then 80.7 percent, band 1.56 then 1.35 C.

LiveWhat the break button teaches4 min▶

Press "Retrofit floor 4, unlogged". The filter keeps floor 4's room estimate close to the readings (0.399 C off), so a tracking dashboard shows almost nothing. But the twin's predictions on floor 4 are now consistently too cold, and the surprises show it: the mean innovation goes from +0.015 C in week 1 to +0.638 C in week 2, a shift test of z = 11.44. The other five floors stay below 0.7. Floor 4's week-ahead energy estimate is 63.1 percent too high, the building's 8.9 percent.

The widget names floor 4 and says what to check: the change log. AIAA and AIA (2020) list "As Maintained" as a phase of the twin's life for this reason: a twin of the building as it was is not a twin of the building as it is. Practitioner sessions 5 and 6 turn this into a routine.

Check yourself

Three questions

Adding the Kalman filter to Kittiwake's calibrated twin (constructed) cuts tracking error from 0.482 to 0.218 C but leaves the setback-week energy estimate at +1.7 percent. Why?

Assimilation fixes where the building is now; calibration fixes how it behaves. A week ahead, the starting state has long been forgotten and the behaviour decides the answer.

Telling the filter the sensors are nearly perfect narrows the 24-hour band from 2.20 to 1.28 C. What happens to coverage, and why?

A band is only as honest as the noise it assumes. The twin also copies every reading (0.403 C error, the sensors' own), so it gains nothing from the physics.

After an unlogged retrofit, floor 4's tracking error is still only 0.399 C. What tells you something changed?

The filter keeps pulling the state back to the readings, so tracking hides the change. The surprises it has to absorb do not. Then check the change log: floor 4's plan is 63.1 percent wrong.

After the capstone

Homework

Thirty minutes on the bench, then on your own work.

Find where the band starts to lie

For each seed, step the assumed noise from 0.4 to 0.01 C and record coverage. Write down the setting at which coverage first drops below 90 percent. Your numbers will differ by seed; record the seed.

Write the readiness note for seed 42

Four lines, in the order of the build-along. Compare it with the canon note and mark what changed.

List your own unlogged changes

For an asset you know, list three physical changes that could happen without reaching any data system: a swapped part, a moved sensor, a new tenant. Who would notice each, and when?

Write the weekly innovation check

One paragraph: which surprises you would average, over what window, what shift would trigger a look at the change log, and who gets the alert.
