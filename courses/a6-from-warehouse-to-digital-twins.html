From warehouse to digital twins: the transfer map

A data team arriving at a twin is not starting from zero, and not starting from where it thinks. Some warehouse skills are worth more next to a physical asset, some ideas are new, and a few habits are actively dangerous. Tonight sorts twelve of them into three columns, names the method, the tool class and the reading for each, and ends with a 90-day self-study ladder on free and official sources. Every Kittiwake Hall number is from a constructed teaching building generated in the page, not observed. Nothing here is investment advice.

The analyst who got the twin ticket, six weeks later

Back at Kittiwake Hall (constructed), the analyst who came from a retail warehouse has a working twin of six floors. Looking back, she sorts what helped into three piles. Her schemas, her habit of slicing residuals and her refusal to score a model on its own training data were worth more than they ever were in the warehouse. State estimation, physics-shaped models and checked uncertainty were new. And three or four reflexes, each sensible in a warehouse, nearly cost her the twin. Real world

The National Academies (2024) report on digital twins asks for verification, validation and uncertainty quantification to be a continual process that adapts as the physical counterpart changes. Read that as a data person: there is no "done" state, no final test set, and the thing being modelled moves. Every item in the third column is a version of forgetting that sentence.

Part 1 · covers the four warehouse skills worth more here

Carries over: what you already have

12 min live

Four warehouse skills arrive intact and become more valuable, because next to a physical asset the cost of getting them wrong is a comfort complaint or a heating budget, not a dashboard tile. The figure maps each one onto the twin record it becomes, using the four records of practitioner session 1.

LiveSchemas, keys and versioning, now for assets, models and predictions3 min▶

The skill. Designing keyed, versioned tables. Here it becomes an asset record keyed on the physical floor (sensors are replaced, floors are not) with a configuration version that increments on every logged change, a model record with every parameter and where it came from, and a prediction row that names both versions. Method: slowly changing records with effective dates. Tool class: any versioned table store. Reading: AIAA and AIA (2020), whose phases As Designed, As Built, As Used and As Maintained are versions of one asset record.

Kittiwake's calibration found floor 4's heat-loss multiplier at 1.42 against a true 1.41 (constructed). That value is a fact about configuration 3 of floor 4, and only that configuration. Practitioner session 1 writes the four schemas.

LiveResidual analysis, sliced by floor and by week3 min▶

The skill. Plotting actual minus predicted and slicing it until a pattern shows. Here it becomes innovation monitoring: each hour, the reading minus what the twin predicted. A twin in sync has small, patternless innovations. Method: per-segment mean shift against a reference window. Tool class: a notebook and a plotting routine. Reading: Kalman (1960), where the innovation is the quantity the whole update is built on.

In the constructed break, floor 4 was insulated without anyone telling the twin. Its mean innovation went from +0.015 C in week 1 to +0.638 C in week 2, a shift of 11.44 standard errors; no other floor moved more than 0.7. A residual plot sliced by floor finds that in one glance. Practitioner session 5 builds the check; formal detectors live in learn anomaly detection.

LiveTrain and test discipline: a calibration week and a check week3 min▶

The skill. Never scoring a model on the data it was fitted to. Here it becomes fitting parameters on one period (Kittiwake: week 1, the normal schedule) and judging the twin on a different one (week 2, the night setback it is meant to plan). Method: a time-ordered split, never a shuffled one; the asset's behaviour has an order. Tool class: a grid search or optimiser in a notebook. Reading: ASHRAE Guideline 14, whose calibration criteria are reported via secondary papers: hourly CV(RMSE) at most 30 percent and NMBE within 10 percent; editions and conventions differ.

Kittiwake's calibration error against the sensors on week 1 was 0.460 to 0.526 C per floor (constructed). That number says how well the fit matched its own week, nothing more. Practitioner session 3 runs the fit and the check.

Self-studyLineage: which model, of which configuration, said this3 min▶

The skill. Tracing any number back to the job and the inputs that produced it. Here it becomes a decision log: what was decided, which prediction it relied on, which model version made it, fitted to which configuration. Method: carry version keys on every output row. Tool class: the same lineage metadata you already keep, plus the asset's configuration version. Reading: National Academies (2024) on continual verification and validation.

After the unlogged retrofit, Kittiwake's whole-building heat estimate for the setback week was 8.9 percent high (constructed). Without lineage nobody can say whether that plan came from a model fitted before or after the change. Practitioner session 8 is governance: versions, owners, who may act.

Part 2 · covers the four ideas with no warehouse equivalent

Must learn: state, structure, assimilation, checked bands

13 min live

A warehouse model has inputs and parameters. A twin has a third thing: a state, what the asset is doing right now, which changes every hour and is partly unmeasured. The figure draws the split on one Kittiwake floor; the four cards name what follows from it; the widget lets you score yourself on all twelve items.

LiveState versus parameters3 min▶

The idea. Parameters say how the asset behaves (how fast a floor loses heat, how much heat its concrete stores). State says what it is doing now (the room temperature, the mass temperature). They are fitted by different methods on different clocks. Method: state-space modelling. Tool class: a state-space or filtering library. Reading: Kalman (1960), which recast filtering and prediction in state-transition form.

In Kittiwake's constructed bench, calibrating the parameters took the twin from 4.450 to 0.482 C off the true room; adding hourly state updates took it to 0.218 C, while the week's heat estimate stayed at +1.7 percent. Good parameters fix the plan; good state tracking fixes the now. Practitioner session 1 separates them in the model record and the hourly row.

LivePhysics-structured grey-box models, and identifiability3 min▶

The idea. Instead of a flexible model with thousands of weights, a small network of heat paths whose shape comes from physics and whose few parameters come from data. Method: grey-box modelling with stochastic differential equations. Tool class: a grey-box or state-space estimation package. Reading: Bacher and Madsen (2011), Energy and Buildings, who grow building models one node at a time and keep extra complexity only when a likelihood-ratio test says the data supports it (method reported).

The new question is identifiability: which parameters can this data pin down? One constructed week found every floor's kU within 0.08 of the truth, but floor 3's kC came out 1.68 against a true 0.89. Fitting a parameter is not the same as learning it. Practitioner session 3 shows why the mass hides.

LiveData assimilation: predict, update, and what the gain means3 min▶

The idea. Each hour the twin predicts, the sensor reads, and the state moves part of the way toward the reading. The fraction is the gain, set by how much the model and the sensor are each trusted. Method: the Kalman filter, predict then update. Tool class: a filtering routine, ten lines by hand. Reading: Kalman (1960), plus any free course notes on state estimation.

Kittiwake's gain on the room reading averages 0.445 to 0.448 per floor in week 2 (constructed): the twin moves a little less than halfway toward each reading. Practitioner session 4 derives it on paper and checks a closed form. Sensors, clocks and missing readings belong to learn IoT data.

Self-studyUncertainty bands checked against outcomes3 min▶

The idea. Every forecast carries a band, and the band is itself a claim that can be checked: a 95 percent band should hold the reading about 95 percent of the time. Method: empirical coverage, per segment. Tool class: the residual table you already have, plus a count. Reading: National Academies (2024) on uncertainty quantification as a continual need.

Kittiwake's 24-hour-ahead 95 percent band covered 96.8 percent of week 2's readings, with a mean width of 2.20 C (constructed): honest, slightly cautious. Practitioner session 7 builds it; forecasting methods in general live in learn time-series forecasting.

Part 3 · covers four habits to unlearn, then the build-along

Must unlearn, then your 90-day ladder

★ 17 min · everyone builds

Four habits that served you well in the warehouse are actively dangerous next to a physical asset, because each one produces a twin that looks fine and is wrong. Each card gives the habit, the replacement and the bench number that shows the damage. Then you build a 13-week self-study ladder on free and official sources, drawn in the figure.

Live"The model is done once it is trained"2 min▶

Replace it with: a twin is refitted as the asset changes, and the innovations say when. The National Academies (2024) ask for validation to be continual and to adapt to changes in the physical counterpart. After Kittiwake's unlogged retrofit, floor 4's 95 percent band held the reading only 69.0 percent of the time (constructed). The model had not changed; the floor had. Practitioner session 5 separates model drift from asset drift.

Live"Trust the latest reading"2 min▶

Replace it with: weigh each reading against the model by how noisy each is. Told its sensors are perfect, Kittiwake's twin copies every reading: 0.403 C off the true room, the sensors' own 0.404 C, against 0.218 C for the honest twin (constructed). The physics adds information that a fresh reading does not. Practitioner session 4.

Live"Narrower is better"2 min▶

Replace it with: a band is only as good as its coverage. The same overconfident twin draws a band 1.28 C wide instead of 2.20 C, and it held the reading 77.4 percent of the time while claiming 95 (constructed). A narrow band that misses one hour in four is worse than a wide one that tells the truth. Practitioner session 7.

Live"Config is not data"2 min▶

Replace it with: the change log is a data source, as important as the sensor feed. An insulation retrofit on floor 4 that nobody logged left the twin planning that floor's heat 63.1 percent wrong (constructed), while its room estimate still looked fine. The AIAA and AIA (2020) As Maintained phase is a record, not a memory. Practitioner session 6.

Real world

Calibration criteria are themselves argued over. ASHRAE Guideline 14's thresholds are widely reported as the test of a calibrated building model, and Garrett and New (2016, reported) question whether such metrics are sufficient. The warehouse instinct to pass a threshold and move on is the habit to drop: a twin that passed calibration last spring has not been checked since.

Mark your three columns

Tick the widget in Part 2 honestly. Copy the list of unticked items onto a page; each one names a practitioner session. That list is the spine of your 90 days.

Weeks 1-3: read the definition at source

The National Academies (2024) report, free to read online (official), Summary and chapter 2 first. Then the AIAA and AIA (2020) position paper, a free PDF (official). Write one paragraph: which of the four tests (one asset, updated with data, predicts, informs a decision) does a twin you know fail?

Weeks 4-6: learn the update

Kalman (1960), the original paper (official), for the state-space framing; then any free course notes on state estimation (free) for the predict and update steps worked through. Do the scalar update by hand once, as in practitioner session 4, until a gain of 0.445 means something to you.

Weeks 7-9: grey-box models and calibration

Bacher and Madsen (2011), Energy and Buildings (official; access may need a library). ASHRAE Guideline 14 is paywalled: read about its calibration criteria in free secondary papers, and note where editions and conventions differ. ISO 23247-1:2021, the manufacturing twin framework, is also paywalled; know it exists and what it scopes. Write down which of your own model's parameters one week of data could pin down.

Weeks 10-13: build, then write the readiness note

Rerun the twin sync bench (practitioner session 10) with your own seed; your numbers will differ and the shape should hold. Then write a one-page readiness note for one real asset: which column each of your team's skills sits in, which sessions they still need, and which of the four habits you have caught in your own work.

The habit to keep. When a warehouse instinct fires next to a physical asset, ask which column it belongs in before acting on it. Most will carry over. The four that do not will each give you a twin that looks fine and is wrong.

Check yourself

Three questions

Which warehouse skill carries over almost unchanged and becomes the twin's early warning?

Reading minus prediction is the innovation. Sliced by floor, Kittiwake's showed floor 4 jumping from +0.015 to +0.638 C after an unlogged change (constructed). Shuffling a time series breaks the order the asset lives in.

One calibration week finds every floor's kU within 0.08, but floor 3's kC comes out 1.68 against a true 0.89. What must-learn idea is this?

A grey-box model's parameters are not all equally learnable from a given week. Fitting a value is not the same as learning it; practitioner session 3 shows why the mass hides.

A team proudly reports that its twin's 95 percent band is now 1.28 C wide instead of 2.20 C. What should a leader ask first?

"Narrower is better" is a habit to unlearn. Kittiwake's 1.28 C band held the reading 77.4 percent of the time; the 2.20 C band held 96.8 percent (constructed).
