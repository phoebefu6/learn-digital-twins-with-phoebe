Calibrating a model to measurements

The drawings say how Kittiwake Hall should lose heat and hold it. The building has its own opinion. Calibration is choosing parameter values so the model, run on its own, agrees with what the sensors actually recorded. Kittiwake Hall is a constructed six-storey teaching building; its numbers come from a seeded engine, not a real site. Tonight you search two parameters on one week of readings, watch the search nail heat loss and shrug at thermal mass, and learn to judge a fit on a week it never saw.

Part 0

From the drawings to the building you have

In practitioner session 2 the grey box arrived with kU 1.50 and kC 1.62 on floor 1, "fitted on week 1". Tonight you open that box. kU multiplies the heat loss the drawings predict; kC multiplies the thermal mass. A search picks the pair that makes a week of simulated room temperatures look most like a week of readings. The answer it gives for one of them is trustworthy. The answer for the other is not, and the search will not tell you which is which unless you ask.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A calibration loop you can write from memory: guess the parameters, simulate the week open loop with the logged heat, score it against the readings, take the next guess. A way to see whether each parameter is actually pinned down: slice the error surface along it and read how flat the slice is. On Kittiwake's floors one week fixes the heat-loss multiplier within 0.08 and leaves the mass loose. And the habit of judging a calibration on a different week from the one you fitted, with the reported ASHRAE Guideline 14 criteria in their proper place.

LiveWhy a twin is calibrated at all3 min▶

A model built from drawings is a model of the building as designed. Kittiwake's real floors lose heat 1.15 to 1.45 times what the drawings say and hold 0.70 to 0.95 of the designed mass (constructed). Run the drawings' model through the setback week and it underestimates the heat the building used by 32.9 percent. Calibration is the step that moves the model from the drawn building to the built one, using the building's own records as evidence. Leader session 3 tells the story of that gap; tonight is the mechanics.

Real world

The National Academies (2024) report calls verification, validation and uncertainty quantification "an area of particular need" for digital twins, and concludes it must be a continual process that adapts as the physical counterpart changes. A calibration is one act of that: it is valid for the building as it was during the data you used, and it expires when the building changes. Practitioner session 5 catches the moment it expires.

Self-studyWords for calibration3 min▶
  • Parameter. A number inside the model you are allowed to change: here kU (heat-loss multiplier) and kC (thermal-mass multiplier).
  • Open loop. Running the model on its inputs alone, never correcting it with a reading. Calibration scores open-loop runs, because a model that is corrected every hour can hide bad parameters.
  • Objective. The single number the search tries to make small. Here the RMSE between simulated and measured room temperature.
  • Warm-up. Hours at the start you do not score, because the model's starting state is a guess (here the first day).
  • Identifiable. A parameter the data can pin down: changing it changes the fit a lot. A parameter that barely moves the fit is weakly identified, whatever value the search prints.
  • Check period. Data the fit never saw, used only to judge it.
Part 1 · covers the calibration loop and the grid search

Calibration is a loop with one number in it

8 min live

Pick a kU and a kC. Start the model at the first reading, drive it through week 1 with the outdoor temperature, the sun, the timetable and the heat the meter logged, and never let it peek at the sensor. Compare the simulated room with the readings from hour 24 to hour 168. One number comes out. Change the guess, repeat, keep the best.

LiveWhy open loop, and why the logged heat4 min▶

Open loop. If the model were corrected by the sensor every hour, as the Kalman filter will do in practitioner session 4, a model with the wrong heat loss would still track the room, because the correction drags it back. Calibration needs the model to stand on its own legs for a week so its parameters are the only thing being judged.

The logged heat, not the thermostat. The real thermostat responds to its own noisy sensor. If the twin simulated the thermostat, its heat would differ from what was actually delivered, and the search would bend kU to cover the difference. Driving the model with what the meter logged removes one excuse. Whether the meter's hours line up with the sensor's hours is a data-link question that belongs to learn IoT data; a one-hour slip quietly biases every parameter.

The result. Every floor's heat-loss multiplier lands within 0.08 of the truth (constructed). The best fits sit at 0.460 to 0.526 C, a little above the sensors' noise of 0.4 C. A fit that went far below the noise would be fitting the noise.

LiveWhy a grid and not a clever optimiser2 min▶

With two parameters a grid is cheap: 16 by 13 coarse points, then 11 by 11 fine points, about 330 one-week simulations per floor, each in a blink. More important, a grid shows you the whole surface. A gradient method would hand back a single pair and stay silent about how sure it is. With five or ten parameters the grid explodes and you need an optimiser, and then you need to ask the identifiability question in Part 2 explicitly, because nothing will show it to you by accident.

Self-studyHow the research does it3 min▶
Real world

Bacher and Madsen (2011, Energy and Buildings 43(7)) model a building's heat dynamics as grey-box stochastic differential equations: an RC circuit from physics, parameters estimated from data. They grow the model one step at a time, adding nodes, and keep a larger model only when a likelihood-ratio test says the data support it (method reported from the paper's record and secondary summaries). That is the same question as tonight's, asked more formally: does the data have enough to say about this extra piece of the model?

The difference from Kittiwake's grid: their estimation treats the model as stochastic and fits it with a filter running inside the likelihood, rather than scoring a noise-free open-loop run. The grid is the version you can see; the likelihood route is the version that scales.

Part 2 · covers identifiability: what one week can pin down

A steep valley and a flat one

8 min live

The search found kC 1.62, 1.00, 1.68, 0.90, 0.80 and 1.20 on the six floors. The true values are 0.85, 0.72, 0.89, 0.88, 0.90 and 0.76. Floor 4 looks fine; floor 3's mass is found at nearly double its true value. Nothing went wrong with the code. The week of readings simply does not carry much information about mass, and the error surface shows it.

LiveReading the two rows4 min▶

Across kU. On floor 3, moving kU from 0.5 to 2.0 sends the week's RMSE from 0.529 C at kU 1.3 up to 7.21 C: a range of 6.68 C, and only kU 1.3 is within 0.02 C of the best. The week has a lot to say about heat loss, because heat loss sets the level the room settles at for a given heat input, every hour of every day.

Across kC. Moving kC from 0.4 to 2.0 changes the RMSE by 0.54 C in total, and every value from 1.4 to 2.0 is within 0.02 C of the best (0.480 C at kC 1.7). The flat run reaches the end of the row, so the valley floor carries on past it. Mass only shows itself in how fast the room drifts when the heat changes, and with a thermostat holding the room near its setpoint most of the week, those drifts are small.

The truth scores worse. The true pair for floor 3 (kU 1.21, kC 0.89) gives 0.589 C, against 0.480 C for the pair the search found. In a flat direction, the search uses the loose parameter to soak up whatever else the model gets wrong: here, for instance, the twin assumes the floors are 80 percent full when the real occupancy runs 60 to 100 percent. A good fit is not evidence that every parameter is right.

LiveWhat to do with a parameter the data cannot see2 min▶
  • Record it honestly. In the model record from practitioner session 1, kC's provenance reads "calibrated, weakly identified". That line is worth more than the second decimal place.
  • Fix it from another source. If the drawings' mass is trusted more than one week's readings, hold kC at 1.0 and search kU alone.
  • Feed it data that excites it. A week with a big change in heating, such as a setback, makes the room drift and gives mass something to explain.
  • Check whether it matters for the decision. Part 3: the twin still predicted the setback week's energy within 1.7 percent.
Self-studyBeyond the grid: methods named, not taught3 min▶

Two slices are the cheapest identifiability test. The fuller versions: a profile of the objective along each parameter (re-optimising the others at every point, rather than holding them fixed as the widget does), the curvature of the objective at the best point, and Bayesian calibration, which returns a distribution over parameters instead of one pair, so a flat direction shows up as a wide spread. Bayesian calibration is named here only; formal VVUQ methods are a discipline of their own.

Floor 6 is worth a look in the widget: its kC row finds a lower RMSE than the search reported, because the valley is not lined up with the grid and the coarse-then-fine search settled in a slightly different spot. On a flat floor, small details of the search decide where you land.

Part 3 · covers judging a calibration and checking it on another week

Judge it on a week it never saw

4 min live

A calibration scored on the week it was fitted to is marking its own homework. Kittiwake's week 2 is the test: the real building turns on a 15 C night setback, a schedule the fit never saw. If the parameters describe the building rather than the week, the twin should still predict it.

LiveWhat the check week says2 min▶

Calibrated on week 1, the twin estimates the setback week's heat at 5,152 kWh against a true 5,067 kWh: 1.7 percent high. The drawings' twin says 3,402 kWh, 32.9 percent low. Open loop, the calibrated twin tracks the true room over week 2 with an RMSE of 0.482 C; the design twin's is 4.450 C. And this with kC wrong by nearly a factor of two on floor 3. For a week-ahead energy estimate, heat loss carries the answer; the loose parameter mostly did not matter for this decision. It could matter for a different one, such as how early to start heating on a cold Monday. Energy figures here are constructed teaching numbers, not investment advice.

LiveThe reported criteria, and what they judge2 min▶

ASHRAE Guideline 14 (editions 2002 and 2014) is reported, via secondary papers, to treat a building energy model as calibrated when its hourly CV(RMSE) is at most 30 percent and its NMBE is within plus or minus 10 percent, or monthly 15 and 5 percent. The guideline is paywalled and was not read here. Three cautions travel with those numbers:

  • They judge energy. CV(RMSE) is the RMSE divided by the mean of the measured values; NMBE is the total bias divided by the total. They are written for metered energy use, not room temperatures, so you cannot apply 30 percent to Kittiwake's 0.48 C.
  • The arithmetic varies. Editions differ, some versions divide by n and some by n - p (the number of parameters), and the sign convention of NMBE is not the same everywhere. Say which you used.
  • Passing is not proof. Garrett and New (2016, ASHRAE Transactions) question whether these metrics are sufficient for calibration (reported). Tonight's floor 3 is the illustration: a fit can pass on its own week and still carry a wrong parameter.
Self-studyOverfitting the calibration week3 min▶

With two parameters and a week of hourly data, Kittiwake's fit stops at 0.460 to 0.526 C, just above the sensors' 0.4 C noise. Add parameters (a separate heat loss for every weekday, an occupancy factor for every day) and the week-1 RMSE will keep falling, eventually below the noise. That is the model learning the week's accidents. The symptom is a fit that improves on week 1 and gets worse on week 2. The defence is the one in the figure: fit on one period, judge on another, and keep the parameter count small enough that each one has something in the data to explain. Splitting time series into fit and check periods properly, with more than one split, is a forecasting discipline: learn time series forecasting covers it.

Real world

Bacher and Madsen (2011) add model complexity only when a likelihood-ratio test says the extra piece is supported by the data (method reported). It is a guard against the same failure: a richer model that fits the training data better and the building no better.

Build-along

Search two parameters and find the flat one

★ 22 min · everyone builds

In practitioner session 2 you searched one parameter and found a clean valley. Now add the second. You will make a synthetic week for a toy floor with a known kU and kC, heated by a thermostat that reads a noisy sensor, with real occupancy the model does not know, then grid-search both parameters open loop and slice the surface. The figure is the grid you are about to fill: 16 values of kU, 17 of kC, 272 one-week simulations.

Write the model and the synthetic week

Make a scratch folder, create calib.py, and paste this first block. The "real" floor has kU 1.30 and kC 0.80. A thermostat reads a noisy sensor and sets the heat, the meter logs it, and the real occupants vary day to day.

calib.py, block 1 of 3# Grid search for two parameters on a toy two-node floor model. import math import random def step(ti, tm, to, q, kU, kC): # One hour, 4 quarter-hour Euler steps. kU scales heat loss, kC scales the mass. Ci, Cm, Rim, Rio, Rmo = 6.0, 20.0 * kC, 0.5, 10.0 / kU, 4.0 / kU for _ in range(4): dti = ((tm - ti) / Rim + (to - ti) / Rio + q) / Ci dtm = ((ti - tm) / Rim + (to - tm) / Rmo) / Cm ti, tm = ti + 0.25 * dti, tm + 0.25 * dtm return ti, tm # A synthetic week. The "real" floor is heated by a thermostat that reads its own # noisy sensor, and the heat meter logs what went in, just as in a real building. rng = random.Random(7) H = 7 * 24 + 1 TRUE_KU, TRUE_KC = 1.30, 0.80 to = [5 + 3.5 * math.sin(2 * math.pi * (h - 9) / 24) + rng.gauss(0, 0.5) for h in range(H)] occ_day = [rng.uniform(0.6, 1.0) for d in range(7)] # how full each day really was def occupied(h): return 8 <= h % 24 < 18 and h // 24 < 5 ti, tm, meas, qlog = 19.0, 19.0, [], [] for h in range(H): y = ti + rng.gauss(0, 0.4) meas.append(y) setpoint = 21.0 if 7 <= h % 24 < 19 and h // 24 < 5 else 19.0 q = min(20.0, max(0.0, 4.0 * (setpoint - y))) qlog.append(q) people = 2.5 * occ_day[h // 24] if occupied(h) else 0.0 ti, tm = step(ti, tm, to[h], q + people, TRUE_KU, TRUE_KC) ti += rng.gauss(0, 0.12)

Write the objective

Append the score. The twin is driven by the logged heat and the timetable's guess of 2 kW of people, never by the true occupancy, and never looks at the sensor until it is scored. Day 1 is warm-up.

calib.py, block 2 of 3def rmse(kU, kC): # Open loop: start at the first reading, drive with the logged heat, never look at the sensor. x_ti, x_tm, err = meas[0], meas[0], [] for h in range(1, H): people = 2.0 if occupied(h - 1) else 0.0 # the timetable's guess, not the truth x_ti, x_tm = step(x_ti, x_tm, to[h - 1], qlog[h - 1] + people, kU, kC) if h >= 24: # skip day 1: warm-up err.append((x_ti - meas[h]) ** 2) return math.sqrt(sum(err) / len(err))

Search the grid and slice it

Append the search. It scores all 272 pairs, keeps the best, then reads the two slices through the best cell: how much the RMSE moves along each axis, and which values sit within 0.02 C of the best.

calib.py, block 3 of 3kus = [round(0.5 + 0.1 * a, 1) for a in range(16)] # 0.5 to 2.0 kcs = [round(0.4 + 0.1 * b, 1) for b in range(17)] # 0.4 to 2.0 grid = {(u, c): rmse(u, c) for u in kus for c in kcs} best = min(grid, key=grid.get) print(f"true kU {TRUE_KU:.2f} kC {TRUE_KC:.2f}") print(f"best kU {best[0]:.2f} kC {best[1]:.2f} RMSE {grid[best]:.3f} C") along_u = [grid[(u, best[1])] for u in kus] along_c = [grid[(best[0], c)] for c in kcs] print(f"along kU RMSE {min(along_u):.3f} to {max(along_u):.3f} C (spread {max(along_u) - min(along_u):.3f})") print(f"along kC RMSE {min(along_c):.3f} to {max(along_c):.3f} C (spread {max(along_c) - min(along_c):.3f})") near_u = [u for u in kus if grid[(u, best[1])] <= grid[best] + 0.02] near_c = [c for c in kcs if grid[(best[0], c)] <= grid[best] + 0.02] print(f"kU within 0.02 C of the best: {near_u[0]:.1f} to {near_u[-1]:.1f}") print(f"kC within 0.02 C of the best: {near_c[0]:.1f} to {near_c[-1]:.1f}")

Run it

Run python3 calib.py from the scratch folder. This is the output from running exactly the three blocks above with Python 3; with the same seed you should see the same lines.

Outputtrue kU 1.30 kC 0.80 best kU 1.30 kC 1.10 RMSE 0.422 C along kU RMSE 0.422 to 9.217 C (spread 8.796) along kC RMSE 0.422 to 0.706 C (spread 0.284) kU within 0.02 C of the best: 1.3 to 1.3 kC within 0.02 C of the best: 1.0 to 1.3

Read the valley, then break it

kU came back exactly; kC came back at 1.10 against a true 0.80, and four values of kC fit within 0.02 C of each other. The spread along kU is about 30 times the spread along kC. Now change things one at a time and rerun: the seed, the true kC, a 15 C night setpoint on two of the nights, the people guess set to the true occupancy. Run it and read the output; your numbers will differ. Write down which change makes kC identifiable.

One line for your model recordkU: {value: 1.30, from: calibration week 1, identified: yes, spread along kU 8.796 C} kC: {value: 1.10, from: calibration week 1, identified: weakly, 1.0 to 1.3 within 0.02 C}

The habit to keep. Never report a calibrated parameter without its slice. "kC 1.10" is a claim; "kC 1.10, anything from 1.0 to 1.3 fits as well" is the truth, and it tells the next person which number not to lean on.

Check yourself

Three questions

Kittiwake's calibration recovers every floor's kU within 0.08 but finds floor 3's kC at 1.68 against a true 0.89 (constructed). Why is kC not identified from one week?

Across kC on floor 3 the RMSE moves 0.54 C in total and every value from 1.4 to 2.0 fits within 0.02 C; across kU it moves 6.68 C. Mass matters to the physics, but this week does not exercise it enough to tell values apart.

Why check a calibration on a different week from the one it was fitted to?

Kittiwake's check week has a night setback the fit never saw. The calibrated twin predicts its energy within 1.7 percent; the design twin is 32.9 percent low (constructed).

The ASHRAE Guideline 14 criteria (reported: hourly CV(RMSE) at most 30 percent, NMBE within 10 percent) judge what?

They are ratios on energy, with edition, denominator and sign conventions that vary. Passing them does not show every parameter is right; floor 3's mass is the counterexample.

Take this with you

Cheat sheet

Calibration. Choose parameters so the open-loop model matches past readings.
Open loop. No sensor corrections while scoring, or bad parameters hide.
Logged inputs. Drive the model with the heat that was delivered, not a simulated thermostat.
Skip the warm-up. Do not score the hours where the starting state is a guess.
Slice every parameter. A flat slice means the data cannot see it; record "weakly identified".
The truth can score worse. A loose parameter soaks up other errors in the model.
Check on unseen data. Fit one week, judge on another; Guideline 14 criteria (reported) judge energy.
Constructed. Kittiwake's numbers are generated; your numbers will differ.
