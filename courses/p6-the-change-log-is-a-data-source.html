LiveWhy a retrofit is a data event3 min▶

Practitioner session 1 keyed the asset record on the floor and gave it a configuration version. That version is not paperwork. The model record says "fitted to config 3", and every parameter in it (heat loss, thermal mass, what a metered kilowatt delivers, where the sensor sits) describes config 3. The day the floor becomes config 4, those parameters are claims about a building that no longer exists. A change log row is the only way the twin can know which of its numbers just went stale.

Real world

The AIAA and AIA (2020) position paper describes a twin following its asset through As Designed, As Built, As Used and As Maintained, and says the twin is updated with data from its physical twin throughout its life cycle. As Maintained is the phase a change log writes: every repair, swap and retrofit is a step in it, and a twin that skips one is mirroring an earlier asset.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • Configuration. The asset as it physically is: walls, plant, sensors and where they sit.
  • Configuration version. An id that increments with every logged physical change.
  • Change log row. One physical change: what, when, who, evidence, and which model parameters it touches.
  • Touched parameter. A model parameter whose value the change makes stale; it must be recalibrated on data from after the change.
  • Effective time and logged time. When the work happened, and when the row was written. They differ when the log is late.
  • Innovation, or surprise. Reading minus the twin's prediction; practitioner session 5 flags a shift in its mean.
Part 1 · covers configuration as data and the change log row

Configuration is data, and every change moves a parameter

8 min live

A change log is usually written for auditors. A twin needs it for a different reason: each row says which model number has just become wrong. The figure follows floor 4 through four changes. Three were logged, and each points at the parameter it moves. One was not, and it points at a parameter the twin still believes.

LiveA change log row, field by field4 min▶

A row the twin can use carries eight fields. Asset id (KH-F4, the floor, never a sensor). Config version, from and to. Effective time, when the work finished, and logged time, when the row was written. What changed: the component, its state before and after. Who: the person or contractor who can be asked. Evidence: the work order, photos, a commissioning sheet. And the field most logs lack: which model parameters it touches.

That last field is what turns an audit trail into a data source. Without it, every change forces someone to guess which part of the model to distrust. With it, a configuration bump can trigger exactly the right recalibration and nothing else.

LiveWhich parameter each kind of change touches3 min▶
  • Insulation, windows, a sealed draught. The heat-loss multiplier kU on that floor. The engine's retrofit is exactly this: floor 4's kU times 0.6.
  • A radiator or valve swap. How much heat reaches the room per kilowatt-hour on the heat meter. The meter still reads; what it means has moved.
  • A moved or replaced sensor. The measurement itself: its offset, its noise, the air it sits in. The model may be fine and the twin will still see a shift. Sensor metadata and its triage live in learn IoT data.
  • A new tenant, a changed timetable. Not a configuration change but an As Used one: it moves inputs (occupancy), not parameters. Log it too, in the input feed, so the twin does not mistake it for a physical change.
Self-studyAs Maintained is a phase with versions, not a status3 min▶

The AIAA and AIA (2020) paper names four life-cycle phases. It is tempting to read As Maintained as one state the asset reaches. For a twin it is a sequence: config 3, config 4, config 5, each with its own date range and its own fitted model. The test from practitioner session 1 still holds: given any hour in the past, can you say which configuration the floor was in, and which model version was fitted to it? If either answer is "probably", the log is the gap.

Part 2 · covers the cost of one unlogged change

What one unlogged change costs

7 min live

Run Kittiwake's twin twice on the same constructed world: once with the floor 4 retrofit happening and unlogged, once with no change at all. The twin's plan is identical in both runs, because the twin never heard of the retrofit. What changes is the building. The error shows up in the plan; the evidence shows up only in the surprises.

Live Kittiwake's twin on all six floors, computed in your browser by the bench engine: calibrated on week 1, kept in sync by the Kalman filter (assumed sensor noise 0.4 C), then asked to plan week 2 under a night setback. "Unlogged" means the real floor 4 is insulated at the start of week 2 (heat loss times 0.6) and the twin is never told; "no change" means nothing happens. Synthetic and constructed: Kittiwake, its weather, readings and the retrofit are generated. True energy exists only because the world is constructed.

LiveReading the two runs4 min▶

The twin's column does not move. Toggle between the modes: floor 4's plan stays at 836 kWh. The twin has no way to plan differently, because nothing it was given changed. The true floor 4 falls from 848 kWh with no change to 513 kWh with the retrofit, and the plan's error goes from -1.3 percent to +63.1 percent.

One floor in six moves the building. The whole-building plan is 5,153 kWh in both runs; the truth falls from 5,067 to 4,732 kWh, so the building error goes from +1.7 to +8.9 percent. Any budget, setback decision or savings claim built on that plan carries the error with it. These are constructed numbers about heating energy, not a forecast of anyone's bill and not investment advice.

It is not a quirk of one seed. Floor 4's plan error under the unlogged retrofit is +73.4 percent at seed 1, +73.2 at seed 7 and +67.0 at seed 42. Your numbers will differ by seed; the shape holds.

LiveWhy the filter hides it3 min▶

The Kalman filter's job is to pull the twin's state toward the readings every hour. It does that job well: with the unlogged retrofit, tracking error against the true room is 0.258 C for the building and 0.399 C on floor 4. A dashboard of live temperatures would look healthy. But the filter corrects the state, not the parameters. Every hour the model predicts a colder floor than the one that exists, the sensor disagrees, and the filter quietly absorbs a surprise of about +0.6 C. The plan, which runs the model forward without readings, gets no such help.

So the evidence is in the innovations, as practitioner session 5 showed: floor 4's mean surprise moves from +0.015 C to +0.638 C (z 11.44), and its 95 percent band now covers only 69.0 percent of readings, against 92.1 percent for the building.

Self-studyWhat the research asks for2 min▶

The National Academies (2024) report concludes that verification, validation and uncertainty quantification for a twin must be a continual process that adapts to changes in the physical counterpart. A change log is the cheapest way to know when the counterpart changed. Without it, validation can only notice a change after it has already cost a plan, and only if someone is watching the surprises.

Part 3 · covers logging as a pipeline

Logging as a pipeline, from work order to model version

5 min live

A change log that depends on someone remembering to email the data team will be late or empty. Treat it as a pipeline instead: the work order is the source, the change log row is the record, and a configuration-version bump is an event the twin subscribes to. The figure is that pipeline, with the step that actually repairs the model in copper.

LiveWho logs, and where2 min▶

The people who change a building are facilities staff and contractors, not the twin's owners. They already write work orders. So the change log should be fed by the work-order system, not by a separate form: when a work order on a tracked asset closes, it must carry the asset id, the before and after, and a "touches" field picked from a short list (heat loss, thermal mass, heat delivery, sensor, none). Make "none" an explicit choice, so a blank never means "forgot". The design of maintenance management systems themselves is out of scope here; the twin only needs that one export.

LiveWhat a config-version bump should trigger2 min▶
  1. Recheck the surprises for that asset from the effective time: did the mean move, and by how much? A logged change that moves nothing is useful evidence too.
  2. Recalibrate the touched parameter on data from after the change only. Mixing pre-change weeks into the fit averages two buildings. Leave untouched parameters alone, so one retrofit does not reopen the whole model.
  3. Version the model: a new model version whose fitted_to points at the new configuration, with the old one kept, so every past prediction still names the model that made it.

Then re-run any plan that is still live. Practitioner session 8 decides who may approve each of these steps.

Self-studyWhen the log is late2 min▶

Logs will be late: the contractor closes the order a fortnight after the work. That is why the row carries two times. Back-date the configuration change to the effective time, re-run the surprise check and the recalibration from there, and re-issue any plan made in between with a note that it replaced an earlier one. A late row is a correction; a missing row is the failure Part 2 priced. Sensor metadata (install dates, positions, replacements) follows the same pattern and is covered in learn IoT data.

Self-studyWhere the standards sit2 min▶

ISO 23247-1:2021 frames a manufacturing twin around "observable manufacturing elements" (reported: the standard is paywalled and only its contents list was read). Read that loosely and a configuration change is something the twin must be able to observe. The standard's scope is manufacturing, not buildings, and no requirement from it is quoted here.

Build-along

Write the change-log schema and join it to the surprises

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You will write a change-log schema the twin can read, then a fifteen-line Python check that joins a toy hourly surprise series to a toy change log. For every week whose mean surprise is beyond 0.3 C, it looks for a logged change that could explain it, and says so, or says "UNEXPLAINED: check the floor". The figure is the join.

Write the change-log schema

One row per physical change, keyed on the asset, with both times and the touched parameters. Copy and adapt to an asset you know.

change_log.yamlchange_id: CH-031 asset_id: KH-F4 config_version: {from: 3, to: 4} effective: (date and hour the work finished) logged: (date and hour this row was written) component: roof insulation before: uninsulated after: insulated who: (work order owner, contractor) evidence: [work order id, photos] touches: [kU] # pick from: kU, kC, heat_delivery, sensor, none recalibrate_from: effective

Write the join

Save as join.py in a scratch folder and run python3 join.py. The toy surprises carry a shift from hour 168 that a logged change explains, and one from hour 504 that nothing in the log explains.

join.pyimport random rng = random.Random(5) # Toy hourly surprises for one floor, 4 weeks: noise, plus a shift from hour 168 to 335 and from hour 504. offset = lambda h: 0.6 if 168 <= h < 336 else (-0.5 if h >= 504 else 0.0) surprise = [rng.gauss(0, 0.4) + offset(h) for h in range(672)] # Toy change log: (change_id, effective hour, what changed, parameter touched). change_log = [("CH-031", 170, "loft insulation added", "kU"), ("CH-032", 400, "sensor recalibrated", "measurement")] for w in range(4): week = surprise[w * 168:(w + 1) * 168] m = sum(week) / len(week) if abs(m) <= 0.3: print(f"week {w + 1}: mean surprise {m:+.3f} C, ok"); continue hits = [c for c in change_log if w * 168 - 24 <= c[1] < (w + 1) * 168] print(f"week {w + 1}: mean surprise {m:+.3f} C, " + (f"explained by change {hits[0][0]} ({hits[0][2]}, touches {hits[0][3]})" if hits else "UNEXPLAINED: check the floor"))

Read the output

This is the exact output of the script above, run with python3. Week 2 is explained and names the parameter to recalibrate; week 4 is not.

Outputweek 1: mean surprise +0.007 C, ok week 2: mean surprise +0.580 C, explained by change CH-031 (loft insulation added, touches kU) week 3: mean surprise -0.003 C, ok week 4: mean surprise -0.505 C, UNEXPLAINED: check the floor

Act on each verdict

An explained week becomes a recalibration ticket for the touched parameter, on data from the effective hour onward. An unexplained week becomes a walk to the floor: an open window, a failed valve, or an unlogged retrofit like Kittiwake's floor 4. Note week 3: CH-032 was logged and moved nothing, which is also worth recording.

verdict to actionexplained by change X -> recalibrate X.touches on data after X.effective; bump model version UNEXPLAINED -> inspect the asset; if a change is found, log it back-dated, then as above logged, no shift -> keep the row; note "no measurable effect" against the change

Break it on purpose

Delete the CH-031 line from change_log and run it again. Then move CH-031's hour to 300. Run it and read the output each time: which week flips, and why does a change logged with the wrong time hurt almost as much as no row at all?

edits to try1. change_log = [("CH-032", 400, "sensor recalibrated", "measurement")] 2. ("CH-031", 300, "loft insulation added", "kU")

The habit to keep. Every row in the change log names the model parameters it touches, and carries the time the work happened as well as the time it was written. Those two fields turn a log into a trigger.

Check yourself

Three questions

Why is an insulation retrofit a data event for a twin, and not only a facilities event?

The model was fitted to the old configuration. In Kittiwake's constructed bench the unlogged retrofit leaves floor 4's plan +63.1 percent too high.

A change log row bumps floor 4 from config 4 to config 5 and lists "touches: [kU]". What should that trigger?

Pre-change data describes the old building; untouched parameters stay as they are; the new version keeps every past prediction traceable to the model that made it.

With the unlogged retrofit, floor 4's tracking error is still only 0.399 C. Why does the filter hide the problem?

The surprises absorb the gap (mean +0.015 to +0.638 C, z 11.44). The plan, which runs without readings, carries it.
